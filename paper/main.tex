\begin{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Motivation.}
Analysis of an immune repertoire begins with germline assignment. A
rearranged B- or T-cell receptor gene is built from germline segments: a V
and a J and, in immunoglobulin heavy chains and in T-cell receptor $\beta$
and $\delta$ chains, a D between them. The segments are trimmed where they
join, and random bases are inserted at the joints. For every sequencing
read, germline assignment names the allele of each segment and locates where
the segment starts and ends. All later steps of the analysis inherit its
errors. The D segment is the hardest to assign: it is short and trimmed at
both ends, somatic hypermutation (SHM) blurs the differences between
alleles, and a base at a joint can belong to either neighbouring segment.
IgBLAST, IMGT/V-QUEST and MiXCR place V and J first and only then search the
bases left between them for a D, which they accept by a fixed match or score
threshold. A V or J alignment can therefore absorb a short D, a real D can
fall below the threshold, and a D can be called on a junction that has none.
These tools are also slow for the millions of reads in a repertoire.

\noindent\textbf{Results.}
We present \swig, an aligner that decides the V, D and J alleles and all of
their boundaries together. Each way of dividing a read into segments
receives one score, a log-likelihood ratio that measures how much better
this division explains the read than random sequence does. Among the
candidate alignments of a read, a dynamic-programming recursion finds the
best-scoring division exactly: it covers every pair of boundary positions
between neighbouring segments. The cost of including a segment is derived
from the number of alternative alleles and positions with which the segment
competes; it is not tuned for benchmark accuracy. Alignments are scored at
the mutation rate estimated for each read. Run forward and backward, the
same recursion yields a posterior probability for every call, and the
reported call is the most probable explanation under these probabilities.

We compared \swig{} 0.39.7 with IgBLAST 1.22 and MiXCR 4.7.0 on simulated
reads, for which the correct assignment is known. From the GenAIRR simulator
we used 13 data sets: two rhesus IGH panels of 50,000 reads and 11 human
IGH, IGK, IGL and TRB sets of 10,000 reads. Every call was scored against
the set of alleles that the read cannot tell apart. \swig{} made the most
accurate D call on every set with a D, and the most accurate V call on every
set except TRB, where it tied with IgBLAST; its J accuracy was within 0.05
percentage points of the best. Its D accuracy was 2.2 and 5.9 points above
IgBLAST's on the two rhesus panels and 0.7--6.0 points above on the five
human IGH sets (0--10\,\% SHM). Against MiXCR the corresponding margins
were 3.6 and 7.0 points and 2.3--7.8 points, even though MiXCR was credited
with every allele tied for its best alignment score. On human IGH reads from
a second simulator,
IgSim, \swig{} called V more accurately than both tools up to IgG-like
mutation levels, and D more accurately on unmutated and IgG-like reads.
Where a junction contained no D, \swig{} still called one on 18\,\% of
IgM-like and 16\,\% of IgG-like reads, against 49 and 31\,\% for IgBLAST and
61 and 39\,\% for MiXCR. \swig{} was also the fastest of the three tools. On
eight threads it assigned about 16,000 reads per second on the rhesus
panels, about 50 times IgBLAST's throughput and 3.3--3.6 times MiXCR's on
the same cores. On four threads, on nine human GenAIRR sets, it was 86--153
times as fast as IgBLAST and 8--13 times as fast as MiXCR.

\noindent\textbf{Availability.}
\swig{} is open source under the MIT licence at
\url{https://github.com/mashu/swig} (crate \code{engine/}). It builds with
Rust $\geq 1.89$, has no dependencies, and compiles to native code and to
WebAssembly.

\noindent\textbf{Supplementary information.}
The Supplementary Information gives the model with its derivations, the
proof of exactness, the search, the implementation and further results.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

High-throughput sequencing of B- and T-cell receptor repertoires (AIRR-seq)
produces millions of reads, each from one rearranged receptor transcript. A
rearranged receptor gene is a mosaic of germline gene segments: a V and a J
segment, with a D segment between them in immunoglobulin heavy chains (IGH)
and in the T-cell receptor $\beta$ and $\delta$ chains (TRB and TRD). During
rearrangement, exonucleases trim the ends of the segments, and non-templated
(N) and palindromic (P) nucleotides are added at the joints. In B cells,
somatic hypermutation (SHM) then introduces point mutations into the
rearranged gene. \emph{Germline assignment} reconstructs this history for
each read: it names the allele of each segment and locates the boundaries of
the segment in the read. The boundaries in turn delimit the junction, which
contains the third complementarity-determining region (CDR3). Clonal
grouping, mutation analysis, lineage reconstruction and the inference of
novel alleles all start from these calls, so their errors propagate.

Three properties make the assignment uncertain, and not merely expensive.
First, the D segment leaves little evidence. Human IGHD genes are 11--37
nucleotides long and lose several bases at each end, so the part that
remains in the read, the D \emph{remnant}, is commonly only 4--8 nucleotides
long. Random sequence matches a few bases of some D gene by chance at many
positions of a junction, so a short remnant is hard to tell from N
nucleotides. Second, a mismatch has several possible causes. Under SHM it
may be a mutation, a sequencing error, or the base that distinguishes one
allele from another. Third, the boundaries depend on one another. An N
nucleotide that happens to match the neighbouring germline can be explained
by either segment, so where V ends, where D starts and ends, and where J
starts cannot be settled one at a time. The task is therefore to choose the
most probable explanation of a read among many partial ones, and to say how
probable it is.

The most widely used tools nevertheless decide the segments one after
another. IgBLAST~\cite{ye2013igblast} aligns the read to the V germlines
first and then searches the rest of the read for D and J.
IMGT/V-QUEST~\cite{brochet2008vquest}, through
IMGT/JunctionAnalysis~\cite{yousfimonod2004junction}, delimits V and J and
then compares each D with the bases between them.
MiXCR~\cite{bolotin2015mixcr} uses $k$-mer seeding and alignment for speed
and likewise aligns D only between its V and J alignments. Each tool then
accepts or rejects a D by a fixed rule. IgBLAST 1.22 requires at least 5
consecutive matching D bases by default. MiXCR 4.7.0 drops D alignments that
score below 25, the score of five matched bases, and keeps further D hits
only if they score within 85\,\% of the best. IMGT/JunctionAnalysis allows
at most a set number of D mismatches, by default 4 for IGH and 0 for TRB.

This design has two weaknesses. Because V and J are aligned before a D is
considered, the local alignment of a neighbouring segment can absorb a short
D, which is then never found. And because a D is accepted by a fixed
threshold, sensitivity to short remnants is traded against spurious D calls
on junctions that have no D, in a way that is hard to interpret. Benchmarks
with a simulator that records which alleles a read cannot
distinguish~\cite{konstantinovsky2024genairr} show that these aligners make
distinct errors and that most of the errors concern D.

Models that decide the segments jointly also exist.
SoDA~\cite{volpe2006soda} aligns V, D and J together by dynamic programming.
iHMMune-align~\cite{gaeta2007ihmmune} and partis~\cite{ralph2016partis} use
hidden Markov models, in partis with parameters inferred from the repertoire
itself, at a much higher computational cost.

Here we present \swig, which treats germline assignment as one optimisation
problem and solves it exactly. \swig{} first collects candidate alignments
of V, D and J alleles to the read. It then scores every way of combining
them, that is, every choice of alleles and every position of the boundaries
between neighbouring segments, with a single objective: a log-likelihood
ratio over the whole read. The assignment that it reports maximises this
objective. The contributions of this work are the following.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{An exact joint decision.} The candidate alignments form a
    \emph{placement graph}. A dynamic-programming recursion over this graph
    returns the best assignment over all combinations of alleles and all
    pairs of boundary positions, in time linear in the overlaps between the
    candidates (\cref{sec:overview}; proof in \cref{S-sec:dag}).
  \item \emph{Costs that are derived, not tuned.} The cost of including a
    segment follows from the number of alternative alleles and positions
    with which the segment competes. Priors on the trimming of a D and on
    the length of the N/P regions rank competing D alleles without changing
    how often a typical D is called, and a further cost applies to a D at
    the truncated end of a read (\cref{S-sec:objective}).
  \item \emph{Read-specific evidence and a probability for every call.}
    Alignments are valued at the mutation rate estimated for each read, in
    exact integer arithmetic. The same graph yields posterior probabilities,
    under which the reported call is the most probable assignment
    (\cref{S-sec:perread,S-sec:posterior}).
  \item \emph{A search with stated guarantees.} Every shortcut that limits
    the candidates is labelled with what it can lose, from \emph{exact} to
    \emph{heuristic}; among the shortcuts that provably lose nothing are
    score bounds that spare \swig{} the alignment of most alleles of a gene
    (\cref{S-sec:search}). The SIMD alignment kernel returns the same
    result, bit for bit, as a scalar implementation on x86-64, AArch64 and
    WebAssembly (\cref{S-sec:implementation}).
  \item \emph{An evaluation on two simulators.} We compare \swig{} with
    IgBLAST and MiXCR on GenAIRR reads, whose truth records the alleles that
    a read cannot distinguish (rhesus IGH panels run through the IgBench
    harness, and 11 human IG and TR sets), and on IgSim reads, with paired
    statistics and negative controls (\cref{sec:evaluation,sec:results}).
    Ablations of every modelling term, a calibration of the posteriors and
    an attribution of the remaining D errors to the search or to the model
    support the comparison (\cref{S-sec:supresults}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Method overview}
\label{sec:overview}

Given a read and germline databases of V, D and J alleles, \swig{} reports
the strand of the read, the alleles of its V, D and J segments (zero or one
D), and the coordinates of each segment in the read and in its germline
allele. These coordinates determine the N/P regions and the junction. With
an optional database of constant-region (C) alleles, \swig{} also reports
the constant region.

\swig{} assigns a read in two stages (\cref{fig:pipeline}). The first stage
is a \emph{search}: it orients the read and collects, for each kind of
segment, a small set of candidate local alignments of alleles to the read.
The second stage is a \emph{decision}: among the candidates, it chooses the
combination of V, D and J alignments, and the boundaries between them, that
best explains the whole read. The decision is the main contribution of this
work, and it defines what the search has to deliver. We therefore describe
the decision first
(\cref{sec:ov-evidence,sec:ov-paths,sec:ov-objective,sec:ov-exact,sec:ov-posterior,sec:ov-constraints})
and the search and its implementation afterwards (\cref{sec:ov-search}). The
Supplementary Information gives every derivation and proof.

\subsection{Evidence: alignment scores as log-likelihood ratios}
\label{sec:ov-evidence}

A \emph{segment hypothesis} is an allele together with the interval of the
read that the allele is proposed to explain. \swig{} scores it by local
alignment with affine gap penalties~\cite{smith1981,gotoh1982}, by default
with $+2$ for a match and $-3$ for a mismatch. Such scores have a
probabilistic meaning~\cite{altschul1991,karlin1990}: multiplied by a
constant $\lambda$, the score of an aligned base is the log-likelihood ratio
of ``templated by this allele, with a certain probability of substitution''
against ``random''. For $+2/-3$, $\lambda=0.634$ nats per score unit, and
the substitution probability that the scores imply, the \emph{divergence},
is 11.2\,\% (\cref{S-sec:scores}). Alignment scores and log prior odds can
therefore be added on one scale, and the objective of
\cref{sec:ov-objective} relies on this.

A fixed pair of scores is a log-likelihood ratio at one divergence only. At
$+2/-3$, unmutated reads are scored under-confidently, because a single base
that separates two alleles is stronger evidence than the scores grant, and
heavily mutated reads are scored over-confidently. \swig{} therefore
estimates the divergence $\phat$ of each read before the decision. It then
values a match at $\ln 4(1-\phat)$ nats and a mismatch at $\ln(4\phat/3)$
nats, the log-likelihood ratios of ``templated at divergence $\phat$''
against ``random''. The estimate comes from the V and J columns that no
assignment disputes: the columns of the best V alignment that lie before
every D and J candidate, and the columns of the best J alignment that lie
after every V and D candidate. The junction bases are therefore normally not
used twice. All values are integer multiples of $2^{-16}$ nats, so that
maxima and ties are exact (\cref{S-sec:perread}). Below, $W$ denotes the
value of alignment columns on this read-specific scale; on the optional
fixed scale, $W$ is $\lambda$ times the alignment score.

\subsection{Placements, cuts and paths}
\label{sec:ov-paths}

We call an aligned segment hypothesis a \emph{placement}. The decision
receives several placements of each kind and has to combine them. A
\emph{path} is a sequence of placements in the order V, D, J with at most
one D. A path may be partial, as when a read is truncated, but it never
consists of a D alone, so the possible paths are V, J, VJ, VD, DJ and VDJ.
The placements are the nodes of a graph, the \emph{placement graph}, and
every such path is a path through it.

Placements on one path usually cannot be kept whole, because the local
alignments of neighbouring segments often overlap: a base near a joint may
match both the end of the V allele and the start of the D allele. A path
therefore also states where each placement is cut (\cref{fig:dag}). Between
two consecutive placements, the earlier one is kept up to its \emph{exit}
position $x$ and the later one from its \emph{entry} position $y\ge x$. The
$y-x$ read bases between the two cuts are N/P nucleotides. An overlap can
thus be divided between the two segments at any point, and any part of it
can be left to neither segment and counted as N/P.

The order of the placements on a path is given by their kind, not by where
their alignments start in the read. This matters when the alignment of a J
allele reaches back over the bases of a D. The path V\,$\to$\,D\,$\to$\,J
then remains available, with the J entered after the exit of the D. This is
the very configuration in which a sequential annotator, which has already
fixed its J alignment, lets the J ``eat'' the D.

\subsection{The objective}
\label{sec:ov-objective}

To choose among paths, \swig{} gives each path a value. Let $q$ be the read,
with positions running from 0 to $|q|$. Formally, a path is a sequence
\begin{equation*}
  \pi=\bigl((p_1,y_1,x_1),\dots,(p_n,y_n,x_n)\bigr)
\end{equation*}
of placements $p_i$, each of a kind
$k(p_i)\in\{\mathrm V,\mathrm D,\mathrm J\}$, entered at read position $y_i$
and left at $x_i$, whose kinds spell one of the six paths of
\cref{sec:ov-paths}. The first placement is entered at its own start and the
last is left at its own end,
$y_1=\operatorname{start}(p_1)$ and $x_n=\operatorname{end}(p_n)$;
consecutive placements do not overlap once they are cut, $x_i\le y_{i+1}$;
and cuts fall on aligned bases. \Cref{S-sec:objective} states the conditions
in full. The value of a path, in nats, is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  v(\pi) \;&=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} w(p_i,y_i,x_i)
     \;-\; c_{\text{start}}(p_1) \;-\; c_{\text{end}}(p_n),\\
  w(p,y,x) \;&=\; W_p(y,x) \;-\; C_{k(p)}
     \;+\; \ind[k(p)=\mathrm D]\,\bigl(\nu\,(x-y) - \tau_5\,t_5(p,y) - \tau_3\,t_3(p,x) + \kappa\bigr),\\
  c_{\text{start}}(p) \;&=\; \ind[k(p)=\mathrm D]\;\copen{V}\bigl(\operatorname{start}(p)\bigr),
  \qquad
  c_{\text{end}}(p) \;=\; \ind[k(p)=\mathrm D]\;\copen{J}\bigl(|q|-\operatorname{end}(p)\bigr),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
where $\ind[\cdot]$ is 1 when its condition holds and 0 otherwise. The first
line sums one term $w$ per placement and subtracts two costs that can arise
only at the two ends of the path. Each $w$ combines the evidence of an
alignment with prior terms, and every prior term is a log prior odds or a
log-likelihood ratio with a stated derivation (\cref{S-sec:objective}). We
explain the terms in turn.

\paragraph{Evidence.} $W_p(y,x)$ is the value, on the scale of
\cref{sec:ov-evidence}, of the alignment columns that placement $p$ keeps
between read positions $y$ and $x$.

\paragraph{Presence cost.} Every placement on a path pays a cost $C_k$ that
depends only on its kind $k$ and reflects the number of alternatives from
which the placement was chosen. A D hypothesis, for example, is one of $N$ D
genes at one of $L$ junction positions, so the prior probability of a D is
spread over $NL$ alternatives. The best of them is a more probable
explanation than no D only if its evidence exceeds
$\ln(NL)-\ln\omega_{\mathrm D}$ nats, where $\omega_{\mathrm D}$ is the
prior odds of a D against none. In score units this threshold is
$\pD=(\ln(NL)-\ln\omega_{\mathrm D})/\lambda$ (\cref{S-eq:pd}). For the IMGT
human IGH set ($N=30$ genes, $L=25$ positions, even odds), $\pD=10.45$:
taken alone, the cost rejects an exact match of 5 bases, which scores 10,
and accepts an exact match of 6 bases, which scores 12. The threshold thus
follows from the size of the germline database; it is not tuned for
benchmark accuracy. V and J pay costs derived in the same way from their own
gene counts. On the read-specific scale, each cost is charged as the value
of $P^{*}_k/2$ matched bases, so that an exact match of a given length is
accepted or rejected alike at every mutation rate (\cref{S-sec:perread}).

\paragraph{D trimming and N/P length.} Four further terms apply to a D only.
They rank competing D placements by how plausible the trimming is that each
of them implies. Without these terms, a short exact match deep inside a long
D allele is worth as much as an untrimmed short allele. Let $t_5(p,y)$ and
$t_3(p,x)$ be the numbers of germline bases that the kept part of the D
leaves out at the 5$'$ and 3$'$ ends of its allele. Under geometric priors
on the trim lengths, with means of 5 and 7 bases, each of these bases costs
$\tau_5=\ln(6/5)$ or $\tau_3=\ln(8/7)$ nats. Under a geometric prior on the
length of an N/P region, with a mean of 8 bases, every N/P base costs
$\ln(9/8)$ nats; each of the $x-y$ read bases that the D keeps is one N/P
base fewer and therefore earns $\nu=\ln(9/8)$ nats. Finally, the credit
$\kappa=5\tau_5+7\tau_3=1.85$ nats refunds the trim cost of a D with the
mean trims of the prior. The priors thus change which D wins, while a D with
average trims is called as readily as without them.

\paragraph{Open flank.} A path may start or end with a D, as it should for a
read that starts or stops inside its junction. Such a path asserts that the
V or the J lies outside the read. This is plausible when few read bases lie
beyond the D, and implausible when many do and none of them resembles a V or
a J. A D that starts a path therefore pays the \emph{open-flank cost}
$\copen{V}(\ell)$ for the $\ell=\operatorname{start}(p_1)$ read bases before
it, and a D that ends a path pays $\copen{J}(\ell)$ for the
$\ell=|q|-\operatorname{end}(p_n)$ bases after it. The cost is the
log-likelihood ratio by which these bases, which show no V or J, speak
against the junction that the D implies. It is zero within an allowance of
24 bases, which was set from simulated junction lengths, and it never
exceeds 6.2 nats (\cref{S-eq:openflank}).

\paragraph{Decision rule.} \swig{} reports the path with the largest value.
Among paths of equal value it prefers the one with fewer D segments, so that
a D is called only when it strictly improves the explanation, and then the
one with the larger germline support, the summed scores of its placements
before they are cut. Alleles that explain the called bases equally well are
\emph{co-optimal}, and all of them are reported. Finally, a read is called
only when its path contains an \emph{anchor}: a V or J alignment that is
significant on its own, with an E-value of at most $10^{-3}$ against its
database. A read without an anchor, such as random sequence, receives no
call (\cref{S-sec:significance}).

\subsection{Exact maximisation}
\label{sec:ov-exact}

The number of paths is large: every combination of placements, multiplied by
every pair of cut positions between neighbours. \swig{} nevertheless finds
the maximum of \cref{eq:objective} exactly. It can do so because every term
of the objective depends on at most one cut. For two neighbouring
placements, the best pair of cuts is then found without trying all pairs:
one running maximum over the exit positions of the earlier placement and one
over the entry positions of the later placement suffice. Processing the
placements in the order V, D, J with these running maxima is a Viterbi
recursion of the semi-Markov kind~\cite{viterbi1967,yu2010hsmm}, in which a
state, here a segment, covers a variable number of read positions. The
recursion returns the optimum over all paths, alleles and pairs of cuts, in
time linear in the overlaps between placements
(\cref{S-thm:dag,S-alg:dag}). The test suite checks the recursion against
an exhaustive enumeration of every path and cut pair. The test reads include
constructed cases on which two more restricted searches return a worse path:
a search that tries cuts only inside the overlap of two placements, and a
search that orders placements by their start in the read.

\subsection{Posterior probabilities}
\label{sec:ov-posterior}

The objective is a log-likelihood ratio, so it also defines a probability
distribution over the possible assignments of a read: the probability of a
path is proportional to the exponential of its value. The forward--backward
form of the recursion, in which sums replace maxima, computes from this
distribution the posterior probability of every placement and the
probability that the read contains a D. \swig{} reports these probabilities
with each call. Because the call and the probabilities come from one model,
the call is a mode of the reported distribution: no other path is more
probable (\cref{S-sec:posterior}).

\subsection{Plausibility constraints}
\label{sec:ov-constraints}

The objective describes a read that contains one rearrangement. On truncated
and chimeric reads, and on reads of a locus that the database does not
contain (cross-locus reads), its maximum need not pair a V and a J that can
form one rearrangement. \swig{} therefore applies three checks outside the
objective: a minimum length for V and J hits (24 and 10 alignment columns),
a rule that decides whether a V and a J can belong to one rearrangement, and
a limit of 225 bases between the two. When the best path fails a check,
\swig{} repeats the decision on subsets of the placements and reports the
best result that passes (\cref{S-sec:guards}).

One further constraint concerns the junction, which is delimited by two
conserved codons: Cys104 near the end of the V and Trp/Phe118 in the J.
\swig{} reports a junction only when the called V and J alignments cover
these codons, apart from a bounded recovery of a Cys104 that lies just
beyond the end of the V alignment.

\subsection{Search and implementation}
\label{sec:ov-search}

The decision is exact over the placements that it receives, so the result
also depends on the search that supplies them (\cref{fig:pipeline}). The
read is first oriented by counting the 11-mers that each strand shares with
the V and J germlines. For V, \swig{} retrieves candidate genes by counting
shared $k$-mers and aligns their alleles in a band around the diagonals on
which the shared $k$-mers lie. Because the alleles of one gene are nearly
identical, it aligns one representative of each group of alleles that share
a coordinate frame, derives bounds on the score of every other allele of the
group, and skips the alleles that cannot rank among the best. Every J allele
of the V's locus is then aligned downstream of the V. Finally, D alleles are
aligned in a window that spans the junction between the V and the J. A D
allele is admitted only if it matches the window exactly over at least four
bases, and a 4-mer filter that cannot miss such an allele spares the
alignment of the others. With a C database, the constant region is assigned
after the decision, from the finished call, so that it cannot change the V,
D and J calls.

No step of this search is exhaustive, so each step is labelled with what it
can lose (\cref{S-tab:search}). An \emph{exact} step returns the optimum. A
\emph{lossless} step provably discards nothing that the model could choose;
the score bounds and the 4-mer filter are lossless. A \emph{bounded} step
discards only what lies outside a stated limit, such as the alignment band.
A \emph{defined} step is a stated selection rule whose output defines the
result. An \emph{approximate} step has a known gap to the optimum of the
model. A \emph{heuristic} step is justified by measurement and not by proof;
the strand test and the retrieval of V genes are heuristic.

Alignments run on a SIMD kernel that aligns many read--allele pairs at once,
one pair per lane of a vector register (inter-sequence
vectorisation~\cite{rognes2011}). The kernel is implemented for AVX-512BW,
AVX2, NEON and WebAssembly \code{simd128}, with portable lanes as a
fallback. Every implementation reproduces the scores and tracebacks of a
scalar implementation of the Gotoh algorithm bit for bit, so the output is
byte-identical across backends, thread counts and platforms
(\cref{S-sec:implementation}). \swig{} reads FASTA, FASTQ or AIRR tables and
writes the AIRR Rearrangement schema~\cite{vanderheiden2018airr} with the
posteriors. It is available as a command-line program, a Rust library and a
WebAssembly module.

\section{Evaluation design}
\label{sec:evaluation}

This section describes the data, the scoring rules and the tool settings of
the comparison. All reads in this paper are simulated, because only for
simulated reads is the correct assignment of every read known; none of the
results is an external biological validation (\cref{sec:discussion}). We use
two independent simulators, GenAIRR and IgSim, which differ in how they
generate reads and, more importantly, in what they count as a correct call.
On the reads of both we compare \swig{} 0.39.7 with IgBLAST 1.22 and MiXCR
4.7.0. The numbers in \cref{sec:results} and in the Supplementary
Information were produced with \swig{} 0.39.7 for this paper.

\subsection{GenAIRR reads and the tie-set score}
\label{sec:eval-genairr}

We use reads simulated by GenAIRR
2.3.1~\cite{konstantinovsky2024genairr} in two groups of data sets.

\paragraph{Rhesus panels.} The first group comes from the IgBench harness
(\url{https://github.com/mashu/IgBench.jl}). We use its bundled
\code{rhesus\_igh} experiment, which simulates from rhesus macaque IGH
alleles. It consists of two panels of 50,000 reads, one with IgM-like and
one with IgG-like rates of SHM under the S5F model~\cite{yaari2013s5f}. Both
panels have a polymerase indel probability of 0.003 and a secreted constant
region placed on the simulated sequence. We call them the IgM and the IgG
panel.

\paragraph{Human sets.} For the second group we simulated 11 sets of 10,000
reads from GenAIRR's built-in human references (seed 7, forward strand). The
IGH reference, from OGRDB~\cite{lees2020ogrdb}, has 198 V, 33 D and 7 J
alleles; IGK has 168 V and 8 J alleles, IGL 181 V and 10 J, and TRB 98 V, 3
D and 16 J. There are five IGH sets: an unmutated set, three sets with S5F
mutation at 2, 5 and 10\,\%, and a \emph{realistic} set. The realistic set
combines S5F mutation at 5\,\% with the artefacts of library preparation and
sequencing: a 5$'$ primer trim of 0--15 bases, a 3$'$ trim of 0--5 bases,
0--1 polymerase indels, 0.2\,\% PCR errors, 0.2\,\% sequencing errors and
0--2 N calls. IGK and IGL each have a 5\,\% SHM set and a realistic set. TRB
has an unmutated set and a realistic set with the same artefacts but without
SHM.

This grid of human sets is the one behind the design analysis of the D
priors (\cref{S-sec:dpriors}); MiXCR 4.7.0 was added to it for this paper.
Because the D trimming and N/P-length priors of \cref{sec:ov-objective} were
designed on its IGH sets, we simulated four of the IGH scenarios again from
a disjoint part of GenAIRR's random stream (seed 50000) as an out-of-sample
check. In both groups, every tool assigns against the same V, D and J FASTA
files exported from GenAIRR.

\paragraph{Truth and score.} A read does not always determine its allele:
after trimming and mutation, several alleles can match it equally well.
GenAIRR therefore records the truth of each segment as a \emph{tie set}.
Each allele earns a point for every read base, within the true region of the
segment in the read, that matches the allele at the same germline index as
in the sampled allele. The tie set contains every allele with the maximal
number of points. The call of a tool for a segment is likewise a set of
alleles, $P$. Against the tie set $G$ it scores the Jaccard index
$|P\cap G|/|P\cup G|$ per read and segment; two empty sets score 1 and a
single empty set scores 0. This is the allele accuracy of IgBench, and the
tables give its mean in per cent. The score rewards a tool for reporting
exactly the alleles that the read supports: one correct allele against a tie
of two alleles scores $\tfrac12$, and so does a call of two alleles against
a truth of one.

\paragraph{Scoring MiXCR.} IgBLAST and \swig{} list all co-optimal alleles
of a segment. MiXCR's AIRR output names a single allele per segment, which
the Jaccard index cannot credit in full when the truth is a tie. We
therefore score MiXCR in two ways. Its \emph{AIRR call} is the single allele
of its AIRR output; this is what IgBench itself scores. Its \emph{tied hits}
are all the alleles whose alignment score equals MiXCR's top score for the
segment, which we obtain by exporting every hit with its score
(\code{exportAlignments}). The tied hits give the fairer comparison with
IgBLAST and \swig, and the one that is more favourable to MiXCR.

\subsection{IgSim reads and germline-equivalent accuracy}
\label{sec:eval-igsim}

\paragraph{Simulator and presets.} \swig{} ships a dependency-free Rust port
of the generative model and presets of IgSim.jl~\cite{igsimjl}. It chooses
alleles uniformly, trims the segment ends as exonucleases do, adds N
nucleotides and random flanks, applies SHM and single-base indels, each to a
preset-dependent share of the reads, and adds instrument substitutions.
Every read base is labelled with its germline origin. Three presets set the
level of mutation. The \emph{easy} preset has no SHM. The \emph{mid} preset
is IgM-like: 40\,\% of the reads carry SHM, at rates of 0.5--7\,\%, and
1-nt indels are switched on for 8\,\% of the reads at up to 0.2\,\% per
base, so that about 2\,\% of the reads carry one. The \emph{hard} preset is
IgG-like: 97\,\% of the reads carry SHM, with a median rate of about 7\,\%,
and indels are switched on for 15\,\% of the reads at up to 0.4\,\% per
base, so that about 7\,\% carry one. To probe the extreme, we also run the
\emph{hard} preset with the SHM rate fixed at 30\,\% on every read.

\paragraph{Germline.} The germline is the human IGH set of
IMGT/GENE-DB~\cite{giudicelli2005genedb}, release 202632-7. It has 480 V, 48
D and 15 J alleles, which \swig{} reduces to 457, 41 and 15 by merging
identical sequences. Every tool receives the set as plain FASTA; \swig{}
locates the conserved codons Cys104 and Trp/Phe118 of the alleles itself.
The simulator draws only V and J alleles whose conserved codon was located
with confidence, because the true junction is defined by these codons: a
truth built on a guessed position would rest on the annotation that it is
meant to test.

\paragraph{Correct calls.} An allele call is correct when it is
\emph{germline-equivalent} to the truth: the called allele contains the
germline sequence from which the segment was simulated, as it was after
trimming and before mutation. No method could separate such an allele from
the true one on the evidence of the read. A junction is correct when it
equals the true sequence from Cys104 to Trp/Phe118. Junction accuracy is
computed over the reads in which both conserved codons are templated, that
is, present in the read as bases copied from the germline: 9,953, 8,130 and
4,667 of 10,000 reads on \emph{easy}, \emph{mid} and \emph{hard}, and 1,441
of 3,000 at 30\,\% SHM.

\paragraph{D calls.} D accuracy alone can mislead. A tool that calls a D
more readily finds more short remnants, but it also calls a D on more
junctions that have none, and it can move along this trade-off at will. We
therefore always report D accuracy together with the \emph{spurious D rate}:
the share of \emph{D-free} reads, those without a D remnant, that
nevertheless receive a D call. We also report D accuracy by the length of
the remnant.

\subsection{Tool settings}
\label{sec:eval-tools}

\paragraph{IgBLAST 1.22.0.} On the rhesus panels, IgBLAST ran with
\code{-organism rhesus\_monkey}, \code{-ig\_seqtype Ig} and
\code{-outfmt 19}, with the GenAIRR J auxiliary file and a rhesus IGHC
region database. On the human GenAIRR sets it ran with
\code{-organism human}, \code{-ig\_seqtype Ig} (\code{TCR} for TRB) and
\code{-outfmt 19}, on \code{makeblastdb} databases of the same FASTA files
and with a J auxiliary file. IGK and IGL have no D, but \code{igblastn}
requires a D database; it was given the IGH D database, which it does not
use for these loci. On the IgSim reads, IgBLAST ran with
\code{-organism human} on custom databases of the IMGT set. Its auxiliary
file lacked IGHJ5*03 and IGHJ5*04; we added them with the frame and CDR3 end
of IGHJ5*01, which agree with the conserved codons that \swig{} locates.

\paragraph{MiXCR 4.7.0.} MiXCR ran as IgBench runs it: \code{buildLibrary}
on the GenAIRR FASTA files, then \code{align} with the
\code{generic-amplicon} preset and floating alignment boundaries, then
\code{exportAirr}. The left boundary floats at the start of the V. The right
boundary floats at the C or, on the human reads, which end in the J, at the
end of the J. MiXCR requires a licence from MiLaboratories
(\code{MI\_LICENSE} or a \code{mi.license} file). On the IgSim reads, MiXCR
ran with the options of the human GenAIRR sets, on a \code{buildLibrary}
library of the same IMGT FASTA. \code{buildLibrary} drops every sequence
that contains a wildcard base. Six V alleles of the IMGT set contain 1--4
unsequenced N bases, and they are the true V of 1.3--1.5\,\% of the reads.
So that MiXCR could call every allele, these bases were filled in MiXCR's
copy from the closest allele. On IgSim, MiXCR is scored by its AIRR call,
because germline-equivalent scoring already credits an allele that the read
cannot tell apart from the truth.

\paragraph{\swig{} 0.39.7.} \swig{} ran with its default calling profile
(R-optimized). On the rhesus panels it started from a prepared reference,
and on the human GenAIRR sets from FASTA files that give the conserved codon
of each allele in its header. On the IgSim reads it started from plain
FASTA, as described above.

\subsection{Statistics, hardware and timing}
\label{sec:eval-stats}

\paragraph{Statistics.} Two tools, or two settings of \swig, are always
compared on identical reads. The command \code{swig evaluate} scores any
table in the AIRR Rearrangement format and therefore treats every tool
alike. For each metric it computes the difference in accuracy with its
95\,\% paired Wald interval, and an exact two-sided McNemar
test~\cite{mcnemar1947} on the reads that only one of the two gets right. We
report the differences and the McNemar $p$-values. Posterior probabilities
are assessed by their calibration: reads are grouped into bins by their
reported probability, and we report the expected calibration
error~\cite{naeini2015ece},
$\text{ECE}=\sum_b n_b\,|\bar p_b-\bar e_b|/n$, where bin $b$ holds $n_b$ of
the $n$ reads, $\bar p_b$ is their mean reported probability and
$\bar e_b$ the observed frequency of the event, together with the Brier
score~\cite{brier1950}.

\paragraph{Hardware and timing.} Every run, for accuracy and for speed, used
one machine: a laptop with an Intel Core i9-13980HX processor, which has
eight performance cores with two hardware threads each and 16 efficiency
cores and supports AVX2 but not AVX-512. The machine had 62\,GB of memory
and ran Linux on mains power. Timed runs were made one at a time. Each
tool's own thread option was set to 1, 4 or 8, and every command was
restricted to the performance cores, so that the three tools ran on the same
cores. On the rhesus panels and the human GenAIRR sets, the tools shared the
16 hardware threads of the eight performance cores; for the IgSim timings of
\cref{tab:speed}, each thread had a performance core of its own. The rhesus
panels were run on eight threads and the human GenAIRR sets on four. Unless
stated otherwise, a timing is the wall-clock time of the whole command:
\code{igblastn} for IgBLAST; \code{align}, including the start-up of Java,
for MiXCR; and the command-line program, including the preparation of the
reference, for \swig. The machine ran a desktop session, and the timed
commands had network access, so their times include IgBLAST's usage report
to NCBI and MiXCR's online licence check.

\subsection{Accuracy on IgSim reads: alleles, spurious D calls and junctions}
\label{sec:igsim}

\Cref{tab:igsim} compares the three tools, read by read, on IgSim reads of
the IMGT human IGH set. The \emph{easy}, \emph{mid} and \emph{hard} presets
hold unmutated, IgM-like and IgG-like reads (\cref{sec:eval-igsim}).

\paragraph{Alleles.} \swig{} calls the V allele correctly more often than
both other tools on \emph{easy}, \emph{mid} and \emph{hard} ($p<10^{-6}$ for
each comparison). It calls the D allele correctly more often on \emph{easy}
and \emph{hard}: on \emph{hard} its D accuracy is 2.2 points above IgBLAST's
and 4.7 points above MiXCR's ($p<10^{-12}$). It calls the J allele correctly
more often on \emph{hard}. The exception is D on \emph{mid}, where IgBLAST
is 0.5 points ahead of \swig; we explain this lead below.

MiXCR's D accuracy is below that of both other tools on every preset: 98.45,
91.42 and 82.25\,\% on \emph{easy}, \emph{mid} and \emph{hard}. Its V
accuracy is level with IgBLAST's on \emph{easy} and \emph{mid} (99.51
against 99.47\,\% and 98.17 against 98.06\,\%; $p\ge0.14$). On \emph{hard}
it is below both, and MiXCR leaves 167 reads (1.7\,\%) unassigned.

\paragraph{Spurious D calls.} The tools differ most in how often they call a
D where there is none. On \emph{mid}, \swig{} gives a D to 18.4\,\% of the
D-free reads, IgBLAST to 49.4\,\% and MiXCR to 61.4\,\%. On \emph{hard} the
three rates are 15.8, 30.8 and 39.3\,\%. At the same time, on remnants of 8
or more nucleotides, the D accuracy of \swig{} is higher than that of both
tools on every preset.

IgBLAST's lead on \emph{mid} is the other side of its readiness to call a
D. Its D accuracy there is 0.5 points higher than that of \swig{}
($p=0.017$), and the whole difference comes from remnants of 1--3
nucleotides, on which IgBLAST is counted correct
for 74.3\,\% of the reads and \swig{} for 36.6\,\%. No method can identify a
remnant this short. Most D alleles contain it and are therefore
germline-equivalent to the truth: on \emph{mid}, 36 of the 48 IMGT D names
on average. A tool that always calls a D is thus mostly counted correct on
these reads, and it pays for this on the D-free reads.

\Cref{fig:operating} shows D accuracy and the spurious D rate together. A
tool can raise its D accuracy by calling a D more often, at the price of a
higher spurious D rate, so a better method lies higher and further to the
left. On \emph{hard}, the default of \swig{} is better than IgBLAST on both
axes: 86.91 against 84.76\,\% D accuracy, and a spurious D rate of 15.8
against 30.8\,\%. On \emph{mid}, the default has a spurious D rate that is
31 points lower, for 0.5 points less D accuracy. The trade-off can be moved
through the prior odds of a D: the calling profile \code{sensitive-d}, which
raises these odds, is better than IgBLAST on both axes on \emph{mid} as well
(94.43 against 93.92\,\% D accuracy, 35.0 against 49.4\,\% spurious D).
MiXCR lies below and to the right of both other tools on both presets, with
91.42\,\% D accuracy at a spurious D rate of 61.4\,\% on \emph{mid}, and
82.25 and 39.3\,\% on \emph{hard}. The other \swig{} settings in the figure
belong to the ablations of \cref{S-sec:ablation}.

\paragraph{Junctions.} IgBLAST reports the exact junction more often than
\swig: for 99.94 against 99.53\,\% of the reads on \emph{mid}, and for 98.33
against 96.81\,\% on \emph{hard}. MiXCR's junctions are exact for 99.72 and
96.29\,\%, between the two on \emph{mid} and below both on \emph{hard}. The
deficit of \swig{} is the cost of its plausibility constraints, which report
a junction only when the called V and J alignments cover the conserved
codons (\cref{sec:ov-constraints}). Without the constraints, \swig's
junctions are exact for 99.98 and 99.66\,\% of the reads
(\cref{S-tab:ablation}), more than IgBLAST's. In return, the constraints
withhold junctions from reads that do not have a complete one. Among the
\emph{hard} reads with a conserved codon that is not templated, \swig{}
reports a junction for 20.9\,\%; without the constraints it reports one for
98.0\,\%, and IgBLAST reports one for 98.5\,\%.

\paragraph{Extreme mutation.} At a fixed SHM rate of 30\,\%, beyond the
IgG-like regime of the \emph{hard} preset, the picture is mixed. \swig{}
keeps a large lead over IgBLAST on D ($+7.3$ points) and on J ($+13.6$
points). IgBLAST is better on the V allele ($+3.4$ points), on the V gene
(95.0 against 89.7\,\%) and on junctions. \swig{} leaves 133 of the 3,000
reads (4.4\,\%) uncalled. For 106 of them (3.5\,\% of all reads) its strand
test chose the wrong strand, because the strand test and the seed retrieval
run out of intact $k$-mers at this mutation rate (\cref{sec:discussion}).
Every read that \swig{} does call is correctly oriented. MiXCR assigns only
900 of the 3,000 reads (30\,\%) and is below both other tools on every
segment. Its low assignment rate is also why it has the lowest spurious D
rate there (25.6\,\%): an unassigned read receives no D.

\subsection{Specificity on reads that should receive no call}
\label{sec:specificity}

A tool should make no call on a read that holds no rearrangement of the loci
in its database. We tested two sets of such reads: 2,000 random reads with
the length distribution of the \emph{mid} set, and 1,000 IGK reads assigned
against the IGH database. \swig{} made no call on either set, because it
calls a read only when its path contains an anchor, a V or J hit at
$E\le10^{-3}$ (\cref{sec:ov-objective}). MiXCR also made no call on either
set. IgBLAST, whose default E-value threshold for saving hits is a
permissive 20, gave a V to 1,272 of the random reads, and a junction to 93,
and it gave an IGHV to 997 of the 1,000 IGK reads. Filtering IgBLAST's V
hits at $E\le10^{-3}$ removes every call on the random reads but still keeps
an IGHV on 470 of the 1,000 IGK reads.

\subsection{Speed}
\label{sec:speed}

\swig{} had the highest throughput of the three tools on every data set and
thread count that we timed.

\paragraph{GenAIRR sets.} On the two rhesus panels, on eight threads,
\swig{} assigned 16,200--16,500 reads per second
(\cref{fig:igbench-timing}). This is 3.3--3.6 times the throughput of MiXCR
(4,600--4,900 reads per second) and about 50 times that of IgBLAST
(320--330). These rates cover only the annotation pass that is scored. On
nine of the human GenAIRR sets, on four threads and with one run per set,
\swig{} assigned 15,400--28,500 reads per second, 86--153 times IgBLAST's
throughput and 8.3--13.4 times MiXCR's (\cref{S-tab:genairr-speed}).

\paragraph{IgSim sets.} \Cref{tab:speed} gives the provisional throughput of
the three tools on the 10,000-read IgSim sets, on one, four and eight
threads, as the wall-clock time of the whole command. For \swig{} the whole
command includes the preparation of the database, which takes 25--28\,ms for
the IMGT IGH set with every allele annotated on load. Each command also has
a fixed cost, which we measured with a one-read run: 0.05\,s for \swig;
0.4--1.5\,s for IgBLAST, or 0.05\,s with its usage report switched off; and
1.7--4.6\,s for MiXCR across our timing passes. On four threads \swig{}
assigns 11,100--11,700 reads per second with posteriors, which is 82 times
IgBLAST's throughput and 12--13 times MiXCR's on the same reads and cores.
Without posteriors it assigns 14,400--14,800 reads per second on four
threads; posteriors add 18--33\,\% to the run time.

\paragraph{Scaling and vectorisation.} From one thread to eight, the
throughput of \swig{} grows 5.6--6.1-fold, that of IgBLAST 7.4--7.7-fold and
that of MiXCR 3.7--5.1-fold. On one thread, the default AVX2 kernel gives
2.3--2.4 times the throughput of the scalar kernel. Every SIMD backend and
every thread count produces byte-identical output (\cref{S-sec:kernels}).

\subsection{Model diagnostics}
\label{sec:diagnostics}

The Supplementary Information tests each term of the model on its own
(\cref{S-sec:supresults}). Its findings are summarised here.

\paragraph{The D priors raise D accuracy.} Switching the D trimming and
N/P-length priors off costs 0.37 and 1.08 points of D accuracy on \emph{mid}
and \emph{hard} (\cref{S-tab:ablation}), and 0.3--3.1 points of the IGH D
score on the human GenAIRR sets (\cref{S-fig:dpriors}).

\paragraph{The open-flank cost separates real from chance Ds on truncated
reads.} On reads that stop at their D or start in CDR3, \swig{} still calls
the right D on 82--94\,\% of the reads. A V followed by 100 random bases, in
contrast, receives a D on 2.6\,\% (\emph{mid}) and 11.2\,\% (\emph{hard}) of
the reads, against 88 and 93\,\% without the cost and the plausibility
constraints (\cref{S-tab:truncated}).

\paragraph{The search rarely loses a D.} With a D remnant of at least 8
nucleotides, the true D is almost never outside the search windows (at most
0.04\,\% of the reads) and almost never covered by the called J (at most
0.14\,\%). The D errors that remain are choices between similar alleles on
short, mutated remnants (\cref{S-tab:derrors}).

\paragraph{The posteriors are well calibrated up to IgG-like mutation.} The
expected calibration error is at most 0.5\,\% for V, 0.8\,\% for J, 1.9\,\%
for the D allele and 1.1\,\% for the presence of a D. At 30\,\% SHM the V
and J posteriors are over-confident
(\cref{S-tab:calibration,S-fig:calibration}).

\paragraph{The constant region is called reliably.} With a C database, the
primary C call is right on at least 98.5\,\% of the reads that show 16 or
more nucleotides of C (\cref{S-sec:constant-results}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

A conventional annotator makes a sequence of local decisions. \swig{}
replaces this sequence with one objective and an algorithm that maximises it
exactly. The results show three consequences of this design.

First, the failure that motivates joint placement, a neighbouring segment
absorbing a short D, has essentially disappeared (\cref{S-tab:derrors}). The
D errors that remain are choices between similar alleles on short, mutated
remnants, for which the evidence is present but weak. Reducing them is a
question of modelling, not of search, and the D priors show what modelling
can gain: two prior terms, each of which depends on a single cut, were moved
into the graph and raised the human IGH D score on GenAIRR reads by up to
3.1 points at no measurable cost in speed (\cref{S-fig:dpriors}).

Second, the trade-off between finding short D remnants and leaving D-free
junctions without a D is set by an interpretable quantity. Every term of the
objective other than the alignment evidence is a log prior odds with a
stated derivation. A user who wants a different trade-off therefore changes
the prior odds of a D, and not an ad hoc score threshold
(\cref{fig:operating}). Two further choices also influence the default
trade-off: the credit $\kappa$, which was chosen on simulated reads, and the
exact match of four bases that admits a D candidate.

Third, the reported probabilities describe the reported call. The posterior
is a by-product of the recursion that makes the call, and the call is its
mode. The probabilities are well calibrated up to IgG-like mutation levels.

The two simulators measure different things, and the comparison leads to the
same conclusions on both. GenAIRR, through IgBench, scores calls against tie
sets that count only the bases a read actually shows. This rewards a tool
for reporting genuine ties and penalises it for reporting more alleles than
the read supports. IgSim scores germline equivalence, and it separates D
accuracy from the spurious D rate and from the length of the remnant.

Compared with IgBLAST, \swig{} is more accurate on V and D on both
simulators up to IgG-like mutation levels. The one exception is D on the
IgM-like \emph{mid} preset of IgSim, where IgBLAST's lead comes from
remnants of 1--3 nucleotides. Compared with MiXCR 4.7.0, the faster of the
two established tools in our measurements, \swig{} is more accurate on V and
D on every set of both simulators. On GenAIRR, a fair comparison with tools
that report co-optimal alleles has to count MiXCR's tied hits. Counted in
this way, MiXCR's D score is 2.3--7.8 points below that of \swig{} on human
IGH and 3.6--7.0 points below on the rhesus panels; its single AIRR call
widens the gap to 10.8--17.5 points. On IgSim, MiXCR's D accuracy is
1.4--4.7 points below that of \swig{} up to IgG-like mutation, and on
\emph{mid} and \emph{hard} MiXCR gives a D to more D-free reads than either
of the other tools. \swig{} is also faster than MiXCR: 3.3--3.6 times on the
rhesus panels and 8--16 times on the 10,000-read sets of both simulators.

IgBLAST is better than \swig{} in two respects. On IgSim it reports more
exact junctions, because \swig{} withholds a junction when its alignments do
not reach a conserved codon. And at 30\,\% SHM its V calls are more
accurate.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{All results are on simulated reads.} Both simulators make
    simplifying assumptions: allele usage is uniform, trims are independent,
    and there are no chimeras or long adapters. IgSim also has no
    hotspot-targeted SHM and no codon-length indels. Several design
    parameters were themselves measured on simulated reads: the open-flank
    allowance from simulated junction lengths, the credit $\kappa$ on IgSim
    reads, and the D trimming and N/P-length priors from an analysis of the
    errors that the previous release made on the human GenAIRR grid
    (\cref{S-fig:dpriors}). The means of these priors (5, 7 and 8 bases) are
    generic values and were not fitted, and GenAIRR's own trim tables did no
    better in the prototype. On human GenAIRR reads disjoint from the design
    sets the priors gained as much (\cref{S-tab:oos}), but those reads come
    from the same simulator. The rhesus comparison (\cref{tab:genairr}) uses
    another species and another SHM schedule. Validation on real
    repertoires, for example from donors whose germline genotypes have been
    sequenced, is the most important next step.
  \item \emph{The comparison is incomplete and the timings are provisional.}
    We did not compare with partis or IMGT/V-QUEST. MiXCR's tied hits are
    derived by us from the hit scores that MiXCR exports. They are not an
    output mode of MiXCR itself, whose AIRR export names one allele per
    segment. On IgSim, MiXCR's copy of six IMGT V alleles has its N bases
    filled in, because MiXCR's library builder rejects such sequences.
    Throughput was measured on one laptop that ran a desktop session and had
    network access. The IgSim timings of \cref{tab:speed} are provisional:
    in a second pass with the cores shared, the times of the same commands
    differed by up to 7\,\% for \swig{} and IgBLAST and by up to 24\,\% for
    MiXCR. On a slow connection, MiXCR's network calls held some runs at
    74\,s whatever their thread count; those runs were discarded. Other
    hardware may rank the thread scaling of the tools differently.
  \item \emph{The plausibility constraints are inherited thresholds.} They
    were taken over from an earlier aligner (\cref{S-sec:guards}) and are
    not terms of the likelihood. The call under them is the best result
    among subsets of the placements, and not an exact maximum over every
    path that satisfies the constraints. On simulated reads they cost
    0.4--2.9 points of junction exactness (\cref{S-tab:ablation}). What they
    give in exchange is robustness on truncated, chimeric and cross-locus
    reads, which the simulators do not produce. A model of truncation and of
    template switching would put a price on what the constraints forbid.
  \item \emph{Spurious D calls remain.} Of the IgSim reads without a D
    remnant, 16--18\,\% still receive a D. On a truncated read, a chance D
    within about 30 bases of the V cannot be told from a real D at the same
    place (\cref{S-tab:truncated}). On TRB, the two D genes are short and
    are rarely trimmed as much as the prior expects, so the credit $\kappa$
    gives D-free reads a D more often.
  \item \emph{Extreme mutation defeats the $k$-mer steps.} At 30\,\% SHM the
    strand test and the seed retrieval lose intact $k$-mers. \swig{} then
    leaves 4.4\,\% of the reads uncalled, most of them (3.5\,\% of all
    reads) because it chose the wrong strand, and its V accuracy falls below
    IgBLAST's. The V and J posteriors become over-confident, because the
    true gene is no longer among the candidates.
  \item \emph{The statistics are approximate.} The E-values use the exact
    $\lambda$ and $K$ of ungapped alignment, which is anti-conservative for
    gapped scores, and the total length of the database, which is
    conservative for near-identical alleles. They gate hits and are not
    calibrated $p$-values. A single divergence per read assumes that
    mutation is uniform across V, D and J.
  \item \emph{Each D allele has one alignment per window.} The decision
    receives the optimal local alignment of each D allele in its window.
    Once the cuts are chosen, the part of this alignment that is kept need
    not be the best alignment of the allele between those cuts. This is the
    one remaining approximation of the segmentation model.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future work}

Three extensions of the model follow from these limitations. First, the
residual error of the D-presence posterior varies with the length of the
junction. This points to an N/P-length prior that takes part in deciding
whether a D is present; the current prior only re-ranks Ds. Second, allele
usage is uniform in the model. Usage priors learned from the repertoire
itself, in alternation with the calls, would matter where one gene
dominates. Third, gapped Karlin--Altschul parameters and an effective
database size can be fitted by simulation. Above all, the assumptions of the
model need to be tested on real data.

\section{Conclusion}

\swig{} shows that germline assignment can be posed as one likelihood ratio
and solved exactly, fast enough for whole repertoires. Four elements make
this possible: an exact semi-Markov recursion over every pair of cuts
between neighbouring segments; priors set by the number of hypotheses with
which each segment competes; alignments scored at each read's own mutation
rate; and posteriors from the same recursion. Every shortcut of the search
carries a stated guarantee, so the errors that remain can be traced to the
step at which they arise.

On GenAIRR reads, \swig{} had the best V and D scores of the three tools
(\swig, IgBLAST 1.22 and MiXCR 4.7.0) on every rhesus and human set, with V
level with IgBLAST on TRB. It had the best J score on every human IG set and
on the rhesus IgG panel. The same held on human IGH reads disjoint from
those on which its D priors were designed. On IgSim reads, \swig{} led
IgBLAST and MiXCR on V and D up to IgG-like mutation levels, with one
exception: IgBLAST led on D for IgM-like reads, and that lead comes from
remnants of 1--3 nucleotides that cannot be identified. \swig{} trailed
IgBLAST on junction exactness and, at 30\,\% SHM, on V. On the same cores it
ran about 50--150 times as fast as IgBLAST and 3--16 times as fast as MiXCR.

\section*{Availability}

\swig{} 0.39.7 is free software under the MIT licence, available at
\url{https://github.com/mashu/swig} (Rust crate in \code{engine/}). It builds
with \code{cargo build -{}-release} on Rust $\ge 1.89$, has no dependencies,
and runs on x86-64 and AArch64 and as WebAssembly. The command
\code{swig -{}-v V.fasta -{}-d D.fasta -{}-j J.fasta reads.fastq} writes an
AIRR Rearrangement TSV. The commands \code{swig simulate} and
\code{swig evaluate} reproduce the IgSim evaluation for any germline set and
for the AIRR output of any tool. The scripts and settings that produce every
number in this paper and its Supplementary Information are distributed with
the source (\code{paper/}); the three-tool GenAIRR panels are run through
IgBench.jl.
