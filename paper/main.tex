\begin{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Motivation.}
Germline assignment is the first step in the analysis of an immune
repertoire. For every read of a rearranged B- or T-cell receptor gene, it
names the V, D and J alleles from which the gene was built and locates where
each segment starts and ends. All later steps inherit its errors. The
assignment of D is particularly uncertain: the segment is short, trimmed at
both ends and flanked by randomly inserted bases, and somatic hypermutation
(SHM) blurs the differences between alleles. Widely used tools place V and J first and
then accept a D between them by a fixed threshold. They can therefore miss a
short D or call a D on a junction that has none, and they are slow for
repertoires of millions of reads.

\noindent\textbf{Results.}
We present \swig, an aligner that decides the V, D and J alleles and their
boundaries jointly. \swig{} scores every way of dividing a read into
segments with one log-likelihood ratio and finds the best division exactly
by dynamic programming. The cost of calling a segment follows from the
number of genes and positions with which the segment competes, alignments
are scored at the mutation rate estimated for each read, and every call
carries a posterior probability. We compared \swig{} 0.39.7 with IgBLAST
1.22 and MiXCR 4.7.0 on reads from two simulators. On 13 GenAIRR data sets
(rhesus IGH; human IGH, IGK, IGL and TRB), \swig{} made the most accurate D
call on every set with a D. On the IGH sets its D accuracy was 0.7--6.0
percentage points above IgBLAST's and 2.3--7.8 points above MiXCR's, even
when MiXCR was credited with every allele tied for its best score. Its V
call was the most accurate on every set except TRB, where it tied with
IgBLAST, and its J accuracy was within 0.05 points of the best. On IgSim
reads, \swig{} called V more accurately than both tools up to IgG-like
mutation levels, and it called a D on 16--18\,\% of the junctions that
contain none, against 31--49\,\% for IgBLAST and 39--61\,\% for MiXCR. On
the same cores \swig{} ran about 50--150 times as fast as IgBLAST and 3--16
times as fast as MiXCR.

\noindent\textbf{Availability.}
\swig{} is open source under the MIT licence at
\url{https://github.com/mashu/swig} (crate \code{engine/}). It builds with
Rust $\geq 1.89$, has no dependencies, and compiles to native code and to
WebAssembly.

\noindent\textbf{Supplementary information.}
The Supplementary Information gives the model with its derivations, the
proof of exactness, the search, the implementation, the evaluation protocol
and further results.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

High-throughput sequencing of B- and T-cell receptor repertoires (AIRR-seq)
produces millions of reads, each from one rearranged receptor gene. Such a
gene is assembled from germline segments: a V and a J and, in immunoglobulin
heavy chains (IGH) and in the T-cell receptor $\beta$ and $\delta$ chains
(TRB and TRD), a D between them. Exonucleases trim the ends of the segments
before they are joined, non-templated (N) and palindromic (P) nucleotides
are added at the joints, and in B cells somatic hypermutation (SHM) later
mutates the rearranged gene. Germline assignment retraces this history for
every read. It names the allele of each segment and locates the boundaries
of the segment, which in turn define the junction and its third
complementarity-determining region (CDR3). Clonal grouping, mutation
analysis, lineage reconstruction and the inference of novel alleles all
build on these calls and inherit their errors.

The assignment is uncertain, particularly for D. Human IGHD genes are only
11--37 nucleotides long and are trimmed at both ends, so that a remnant of
4--8 nucleotides is common. A stretch this short also matches some D gene by
chance at many positions of a junction. SHM adds
to the difficulty, because a mismatch may be a mutation, a sequencing error
or the base that distinguishes two alleles. Finally, the boundaries of the
segments cannot be placed independently of one another: an N nucleotide that
happens to match the neighbouring germline can be attributed to either
segment. An aligner therefore has to weigh many partial explanations of a
read against each other, and it should report how confident it is in the one
that it chooses.

Widely used aligners nevertheless treat the segments one after another.
IgBLAST~\cite{ye2013igblast} aligns the read to the V germlines first and
then searches the rest of the read for D and J.
IMGT/V-QUEST~\cite{brochet2008vquest}, through
IMGT/JunctionAnalysis~\cite{yousfimonod2004junction}, delimits V and J and
then compares each D with the bases between them.
MiXCR~\cite{bolotin2015mixcr}, which uses $k$-mer seeding for speed,
likewise aligns D only between its V and J alignments. Each of these tools
accepts a D by a fixed rule: a minimum number of consecutive matches, a
minimum alignment score or a maximum number of mismatches. This design has
two consequences. The local alignment of a neighbouring segment, which is
fixed first, can absorb a short D. And a fixed threshold trades the
detection of short D remnants against spurious D calls on junctions that
contain no D, in a way that is hard to interpret. Methods that decide the
segments jointly also exist. SoDA~\cite{volpe2006soda} aligns V, D and J
together by dynamic programming, and iHMMune-align~\cite{gaeta2007ihmmune}
and partis~\cite{ralph2016partis} use hidden Markov models, in partis with
parameters inferred from the repertoire itself, at a much higher
computational cost. Benchmarks with a simulator that records which alleles a
read cannot distinguish~\cite{konstantinovsky2024genairr} show that these
aligners make distinct errors, most of them in D.

Here we present \swig, an aligner that makes the joint decision exactly and
is fast enough for whole repertoires. \swig{} collects candidate alignments
for V, D and J and then evaluates every way of combining them, including
every position of the boundaries between neighbouring segments, under one
likelihood-ratio score. A dynamic-programming recursion finds the best
combination, the cost of calling a segment follows from the number of
alternatives with which the segment competes, and every call is reported
with a posterior probability.
We outline the approach in \cref{sec:overview} and leave its derivations and
proofs to the Supplementary Information. The larger part of this paper
(\cref{sec:results}) compares \swig{} with IgBLAST and MiXCR on simulated
reads from two independent simulators. We examine the accuracy of the calls,
D calls where no D is present, reads that should receive no call, and speed.

\section{Approach}
\label{sec:overview}

\swig{} takes a read and germline databases of V, D and J alleles. It
reports the strand of the read, the alleles of its segments, and the
coordinates of each segment in the read and in the germline, from which the
N/P regions and the junction follow. With a database of constant-region (C)
alleles it also reports the constant region.

A read is assigned in two stages (\cref{fig:pipeline}). The search stage
orients the read and collects a small number of candidate local
alignments~\cite{smith1981,gotoh1982}, which we call placements, for each
kind of segment. The decision stage then
chooses, among these candidates, the V, D and J alignments and the
boundaries between them that together explain the read best. It is the
decision stage that distinguishes \swig{} from sequential aligners.

The decision is joint because candidate alignments cannot simply be accepted
as they are. Alignments of neighbouring segments can overlap, since a base
near a joint can match both the end of one germline segment and the start of
the next. In \swig{}, an assignment therefore consists of at most
one candidate for each of V, D and J together with the positions at which
each candidate is cut, and the read bases between two cuts are N/P
nucleotides (\cref{fig:dag}). An overlap can thus go to either segment, be
split between them at any point, or be left to neither. Candidates are
ordered by the kind of segment and not by their position in the read. A D
can therefore be placed even when the alignment of a J reaches back over its
bases, which is exactly the situation in which a sequential aligner lets the
J absorb the D. An assignment may also lack a V or a J, as a truncated read
requires.

Every assignment receives one score, a log-likelihood ratio that measures
how much better the assignment explains the read than random sequence does.
Alignment scores are themselves log-likelihood
ratios~\cite{altschul1991,karlin1990}, so the evidence of the segments and
the prior terms can be added on one scale. The most important prior term is a cost that each segment pays for
being included. This cost is not a tuned threshold. It follows from the
number of alternatives: a D, for example, could be any of the D genes at any
position of the junction, and among that many alternatives a short match is
likely to arise by chance. For the human IGH germline used below, the cost
on its own is equivalent to an alignment score of 10.45. An exact match of
five bases, which scores 10, is therefore rejected, and an exact match of
six bases, which scores 12, is accepted. Two further priors, on how far the
ends of a D are trimmed and on the length of the N/P regions, rank competing
D alleles. Without them, a short exact match deep inside a long D gene would
count as much as an untrimmed short gene. A last term applies to a D at the
open end of a truncated read, which is charged for the V or J that the
remaining bases of the read fail to show (\cref{S-sec:objective}).

The evidence itself is adapted to each read. Fixed match and mismatch scores
are log-likelihood ratios at one mutation rate only; the common $+2/-3$
scores correspond to a divergence of 11.2\,\% between read and germline.
Applied to all reads, they understate what a single differing base says
about the allele in an unmutated read and overstate it in a heavily mutated
one. \swig{} therefore estimates the divergence of each read from those
parts of its V and J alignments that no competing candidate claims, and it
weighs matches and mismatches accordingly (\cref{S-sec:perread}).

Although the number of possible assignments is large, the best one is found
exactly. Every term of the score depends on at most one cut. This allows a
dynamic-programming recursion of the semi-Markov Viterbi
type~\cite{viterbi1967,yu2010hsmm} to cover every combination of candidates
and every pair of cuts, in time linear in the overlaps between the
candidates (\cref{S-thm:dag,S-alg:dag}). Because the score is a log-likelihood ratio,
it also defines a probability distribution over assignments. The
sum-product form of the same recursion yields the posterior probability of
each called segment and of the presence of a D, and the reported call is the
most probable assignment under this distribution (\cref{S-sec:posterior}).

Two safeguards act outside the score. First, a read is called only if it
contains a V or J alignment that is significant on its own, with an E-value
of at most $10^{-3}$, so that chance calls on random sequence are rare
(\cref{S-sec:significance}). Second, the score assumes that a read contains
one rearrangement, which truncated, chimeric and cross-locus reads violate. \swig{} therefore requires minimum lengths of the V and J
alignments, a plausible pairing of the two and a limit on the distance
between them. If the best assignment fails these checks, the decision is
repeated on subsets of the candidates, and the best result that passes is
reported. A junction is reported only when the called alignments cover the
two conserved codons that delimit it, Cys104 and Trp/Phe118
(\cref{S-sec:guards}).

The candidates come from a conventional search: $k$-mer retrieval and banded
alignment for V, alignment of every J allele of the V's locus downstream of
the V, and alignment of the D alleles in a window around the junction. The
Supplementary Information states for each shortcut of this search whether it
can lose a candidate (\cref{S-sec:search}). Alignments are computed with
SIMD instructions for many read--allele pairs at once~\cite{rognes2011}, and
the output is identical across instruction sets, thread counts and
platforms. \swig{} reads FASTA, FASTQ and AIRR tables and writes the AIRR
Rearrangement format~\cite{vanderheiden2018airr}; it is available as a
command-line program, a Rust library and a WebAssembly module
(\cref{S-sec:implementation}).

\subsection{Benchmark design}
\label{sec:evaluation}

We compared \swig{} 0.39.7 with IgBLAST 1.22 and MiXCR 4.7.0 on simulated
reads, for which the correct assignment of every read is known. We used two
independent simulators, because they define a correct call in different
ways. \Cref{S-sec:protocol} describes the data sets, the settings of the
tools and the statistics in full.

GenAIRR~\cite{konstantinovsky2024genairr} records the truth of each segment
as a tie set: the alleles that best match the bases of the read in that
segment, several when the read cannot tell them apart. A call, which may
itself name several
alleles, is scored by the Jaccard index between the called set and the tie
set. A tool is thus rewarded for reporting exactly the alleles that the read
supports, and we report the mean of this score in per cent. From GenAIRR we
used two rhesus macaque IGH panels of 50,000 reads, with IgM-like and
IgG-like levels of SHM, which were run through the IgBench harness
(\url{https://github.com/mashu/IgBench.jl}). We also used 11 human sets of
10,000 reads: five IGH sets (no SHM; 2, 5 and 10\,\% SHM; and a
\emph{realistic} set that adds library and sequencing artefacts to 5\,\%
SHM) and two sets each for IGK, IGL and TRB. IgBLAST and \swig{} report all
alleles that are tied for a segment, whereas the AIRR output of MiXCR names
a single one. We therefore score MiXCR in two ways: by its AIRR call, and by
its tied hits, that is, all alleles whose alignment score equals its best.

The second simulator is IgSim~\cite{igsimjl}, of which \swig{} includes a
port. We used it with the human IGH germline of
IMGT/GENE-DB~\cite{giudicelli2005genedb} and three presets, \emph{easy}
(no SHM), \emph{mid} (IgM-like) and \emph{hard} (IgG-like), and with an
additional set in which every read carries 30\,\% SHM. Here an allele call
is correct when it is germline-equivalent to the truth: the called allele
contains the germline sequence from which the segment was simulated, so that
no method could tell the two apart. A tool can raise its D accuracy on such
reads simply by calling a D more often. We therefore report D accuracy
together with the spurious D rate, the share of the reads without any
remnant of a D (D-free reads) that nevertheless receive a D call. On IgSim
reads the tools are compared read by read, and differences in accuracy are
tested with an exact McNemar test~\cite{mcnemar1947}. All differences below
are given in percentage points.

\subsection{Allele accuracy on GenAIRR reads}
\label{sec:genairr}

On the two rhesus panels, \swig{} had the highest V and D scores of the
three tools (\cref{tab:genairr,fig:genairr}). The difference was largest for
D and grew with mutation: \swig{} was 2.2 points above IgBLAST on the IgM
panel and 5.9 points above on the IgG panel. Its J score was 0.04 points
below IgBLAST's on IgM and 0.4 points above it on IgG. For MiXCR the result
depends on the scoring. Its AIRR call names one allele per segment, which
earns at most $\tfrac12$ against a tie of two alleles, and by this score
MiXCR was 14.7 and 17.5 points below \swig{} on D. With its tied hits MiXCR
reached D scores of 94.45 and 86.20\,\%, which is still 3.6 and 7.0 points
below \swig{} and 1.4 and 1.2 points below IgBLAST. On V the tied hits came
within 0.1 points of \swig, and on J they were level with both tools on IgM
and between them on IgG. A tie-aware truth thus penalises a single-allele
call heavily, and the tied hits are the fairer basis for comparison.

(\cref{fig:genairr-human}): over IgBLAST it rose from 0.7 points on
unmutated reads to 6.0 points at 10\,\% SHM, and over MiXCR's tied hits from
2.3 to 7.8 points. On the realistic set the leads were 2.4 and 4.2 points.
MiXCR's AIRR call scored lower still (IGH D 74.6--87.7\,\%, V
95.1--99.5\,\%). On TRB, \swig{} had the best D score (99.9 and
99.5\,\%, against at most 98.2\,\% for the other tools) and the same V score
as IgBLAST, but its J score was 0.04 and 0.05 points lower. The cause is ten
reads in each TRB set whose J is trimmed 31--42 bases into its germline,
past its Trp/Phe codon, so that only 8--19 bases of the J remain. Such a
remnant falls below the minimum J length of \swig{} or fails its V--J
pairing rule, and no J is called (\cref{S-sec:guards}).

The priors that rank D alleles were designed on the IGH sets of this
benchmark, so these sets could favour \swig. We therefore repeated four IGH
scenarios with reads from a disjoint part of the simulator's random stream.
\swig{} again led both tools on V, D and J in every scenario, with a D score
0.9--6.3 points above IgBLAST's and 2.0--7.7 points above that of MiXCR's
tied hits (\cref{S-tab:oos}).

\subsection{Accuracy on IgSim reads}
\label{sec:igsim}

On IgSim reads, \swig{} called the V allele correctly more often than both
other tools on \emph{easy}, \emph{mid} and \emph{hard} (\cref{tab:igsim};
$p<10^{-6}$ for each comparison). It also called the D allele correctly more
often on \emph{easy} and \emph{hard}, by 2.2 points over IgBLAST and 4.7
points over MiXCR on \emph{hard} ($p<10^{-12}$), and the J allele on
\emph{hard}. MiXCR had the lowest D accuracy on every preset (98.45, 91.42
and 82.25\,\%), 1.4--4.7 points below \swig. Its V accuracy was level with
IgBLAST's on \emph{easy} and \emph{mid} ($p\ge0.14$) and below both tools on
\emph{hard}, where it left 167 reads (1.7\,\%) unassigned.

The tools differed widely on reads that contain no D. \swig{} gave a D to
18.4\,\% of the D-free reads on \emph{mid} and to 15.8\,\% on \emph{hard}.
IgBLAST gave one to 49.4 and 30.8\,\%, and MiXCR to 61.4 and 39.3\,\%. This
readiness to call a D explains the one case in which IgBLAST was ahead on D.
On \emph{mid} its D accuracy was 0.5 points above that of \swig{}
($p=0.017$). The whole difference came from remnants of 1--3 nucleotides,
for which IgBLAST was counted correct on 74.3\,\% of the reads and \swig{}
on 36.6\,\%. A remnant this short cannot be identified. It is contained in
most D alleles (on \emph{mid}, in 36 of the 48 IMGT D names on average), and
all of these are germline-equivalent to the truth. A tool that always calls
a D is therefore usually counted correct. On remnants of 8 or
more nucleotides, \swig{} had the highest D accuracy on every preset.

\Cref{fig:operating} shows D accuracy and the spurious D rate together. On
\emph{hard}, the default setting of \swig{} was better than IgBLAST on both
measures (86.91 against 84.76\,\% D accuracy, at a spurious D rate of 15.8
against 30.8\,\%). On \emph{mid} it had a spurious D rate that was 31 points
lower, for 0.5 points less D accuracy. Raising the prior odds of a D, as the
calling profile \code{sensitive-d} does, made \swig{} better than IgBLAST on
both measures on \emph{mid} as well (94.43 against 93.92\,\% and 35.0
against 49.4\,\%). MiXCR was worse than both other tools on both measures on
both presets.

\subsection{Reads that should receive no call}
\label{sec:specificity}

A tool should make no call on a read that holds no rearrangement of the loci
in its database. We tested this with 2,000 random reads that have the length
distribution of the \emph{mid} set, and with 1,000 IGK reads assigned
against the IGH database. \swig{} and MiXCR made no call on either set.
IgBLAST's default E-value threshold for saving hits is a permissive 20. It
gave a V to 1,272 of the random reads and a junction to 93, and it gave an
IGHV to 997 of the 1,000 IGK reads. Filtering IgBLAST's V hits at
$E\le10^{-3}$, the threshold that \swig{} applies, removed every call on the
random reads but still left an IGHV on 470 of the 1,000 IGK reads.

\swig{} was the fastest of the three tools in every timing that we report. On
the
rhesus panels it assigned 16,200--16,500 reads per second on eight threads,

\subsection{Contribution of the model's components}
\label{sec:diagnostics}

Further experiments isolate what individual parts of the model contribute
(\cref{S-sec:supresults}). Without the two priors that rank D alleles, D
accuracy on IgSim reads fell by 0.37 points on \emph{mid} and 1.08 points on
\emph{hard} (\cref{S-tab:ablation}), and the IGH D score on the human
GenAIRR sets fell by 0.3--3.1 points (\cref{S-fig:dpriors}). On truncated
reads, the cost for a D at the open end of a read acts as intended. On reads
that stop at their D or start in CDR3, the right D was still called on
82--94\,\% of the reads. A V followed by 100 random bases, in contrast,
received a D on 2.6\,\% (\emph{mid}) and 11.2\,\% (\emph{hard}) of the
reads, against 88 and 93\,\% without this cost and the plausibility checks
(\cref{S-tab:truncated}).

An attribution of the remaining D errors shows where they arise. With a
remnant of at least 8 nucleotides, the true D lay outside the search windows
on at most 0.04\,\% of the reads and was covered by the called J on at most
0.14\,\%. The remaining errors were choices between similar alleles on
short, mutated remnants (\cref{S-tab:derrors}). The posterior probabilities
were well calibrated up to IgG-like mutation, with an expected calibration
error of at most 0.5\,\% for V, 0.8\,\% for J, 1.9\,\% for the D allele and
1.1\,\% for the presence of a D. At 30\,\% SHM the V and J posteriors were
over-confident (\cref{S-tab:calibration,S-fig:calibration}). With a C database, the primary
C call was right on at least 98.5\,\% of the reads that show 16 or more
nucleotides of C (\cref{S-sec:constant-results}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\swig{} replaces the sequence of local decisions that a conventional aligner
makes with one score and its exact maximum. The first consequence is that
the failure which motivates joint placement, a neighbouring segment
absorbing a short D, has essentially disappeared from its calls
(\cref{S-tab:derrors}). The D errors that remain are choices between similar
alleles on short, mutated remnants, for which the evidence itself is weak.
Further gains must therefore come from better modelling and not from better
search. The priors that rank D alleles illustrate this: two terms of the
score raised the human IGH D score on GenAIRR reads by up to 3.1 points, at
no measurable cost in speed (\cref{S-fig:dpriors}).

The second consequence is that the trade-off between finding short D
remnants and leaving D-free junctions without a D becomes interpretable.
Sequential tools fix this trade-off with a match or score threshold. On the
IgSim reads, IgBLAST and MiXCR called a D on 31--61\,\% of the junctions
that contain none. A tool that calls a D this readily is also counted
correct on remnants that are too short to identify, as IgBLAST's lead on the
IgM-like reads showed. In \swig{} the trade-off is governed by the prior
odds that a junction contains a D, a quantity that has a meaning and that a
user can change (\cref{fig:operating}). Two empirical choices remain in the
default: a credit in the trimming prior that was chosen on simulated reads,
and the requirement that a D candidate contain an exact match of four bases.
The third consequence concerns the reported probabilities. They come from
the same recursion as the call, so they describe the call that is reported,
and they were well calibrated up to IgG-like levels of mutation.

It matters that two simulators were used, because they define truth
differently. GenAIRR's tie sets count only the bases that a read actually
shows, which rewards a tool for reporting genuine ties and penalises it for
reporting more alleles than the read supports. IgSim's germline equivalence
separates D accuracy from the spurious D rate and from the length of the
remnant. On both, \swig{} was more accurate than IgBLAST on V and D up to
IgG-like mutation, except for D on the IgM-like IgSim reads, where
IgBLAST's lead came from remnants of 1--3 nucleotides. \swig{} was more
accurate than MiXCR on V and D on every set of both simulators. With MiXCR's
single AIRR call instead of its tied hits, the D gap on GenAIRR widened to
10.8--17.5 points. IgBLAST reported more exact junctions on
IgSim, because \swig{} withholds a junction when its alignments do not reach
a conserved codon, and its V calls were more accurate at 30\,\% SHM.

These conclusions have limits. All reads were simulated, and both simulators
simplify the biology: allele usage is uniform, trims are independent, and
there are no chimeras or long adapters. Several parameters of \swig{} were
themselves set on simulated reads, and although the priors that rank D
alleles gained as much on reads on which they were not designed, those reads
came from the same simulator. Whether the advantages carry over to real
repertoires therefore remains to be shown, for example with donors whose
germline genotypes have been sequenced. The comparison is also incomplete:
we did not include partis or IMGT/V-QUEST, MiXCR's tied hits are our
reconstruction from the scores that it exports, and all timings come from a
single laptop. Finally, \swig{} has weaknesses of its own. It still gives a
D to 16--18\,\% of the D-free IgSim reads. Its plausibility checks are
thresholds outside the model, and they cost 0.4--2.9 points of exact
junctions. And at 30\,\% SHM it leaves 4.4\,\% of the reads uncalled, is
less accurate than IgBLAST on V and reports over-confident V and J
posteriors. \Cref{S-sec:limitations} treats these points in more detail.

Three extensions follow from these results. The remaining miscalibration of
the D-presence probability varies with the length of the junction. This
points to an N/P-length prior that takes part in deciding whether a D is
present; the current prior only ranks Ds. Allele usage is uniform in the
model, and usage priors learned from the repertoire itself, in alternation
with the calls, would matter where one gene dominates. Gapped
Karlin--Altschul parameters and an effective database size can be fitted by
simulation. Above all, the assumptions of the model need to be tested on
real data.

\section{Conclusion}

Germline assignment can be posed as the maximisation of one likelihood ratio
and solved exactly, fast enough for whole repertoires. On simulated reads,
\swig{} called D more accurately than IgBLAST and MiXCR on every GenAIRR
data set with a D, and it gave a D to far fewer junctions that contain none.
Its V calls were at least as accurate up to IgG-like levels of mutation.
IgBLAST
remained ahead in junction exactness and, at 30\,\% SHM, in V. On the same
cores \swig{} ran about 50--150 times as fast as IgBLAST and 3--16 times as
fast as MiXCR.

\section*{Availability}

\swig{} 0.39.7 is free software under the MIT licence, available at
\url{https://github.com/mashu/swig} (Rust crate in \code{engine/}). It builds
with \code{cargo build -{}-release} on Rust $\ge 1.89$, has no dependencies,
and runs on x86-64 and AArch64 and as WebAssembly. The command
\code{swig -{}-v V.fasta -{}-d D.fasta -{}-j J.fasta reads.fastq} writes an
AIRR Rearrangement TSV, and \code{swig simulate} and \code{swig evaluate}
reproduce the IgSim evaluation for any germline set and for the AIRR output
of any tool. The scripts behind the results of this paper are distributed
with the source (\code{paper/}); the three-tool GenAIRR panels are run
through IgBench.jl.
