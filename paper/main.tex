\begin{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Motivation.}
A rearranged adaptive immune receptor gene, from a B or a T cell, is built
from germline segments: a V and a J, and, in immunoglobulin heavy chains and
T-cell receptor $\beta$ and $\delta$ chains, a D between them. Each segment
is trimmed at the joints, and randomly inserted bases separate them.
Germline assignment names the allele of each segment and locates where it
starts and ends. It is the first step of repertoire analysis, and every
later step inherits its errors. D assignment is uncertain: the segment is
short and trimmed at both ends, somatic hypermutation (SHM) blurs the
differences between alleles, and a junction base can belong to either
neighbouring segment. An aligner can over-call a D on a junction that has
none, and it can miss a D that is present: IgBLAST, IMGT/V-QUEST and MiXCR
place V and J first and only then search the bases left between them, so a
V or J alignment can absorb a short remnant, and a remnant can fail a fixed
match or score threshold. Either error leaves a less accurate D assignment
for downstream methods. The same tools are slow for the millions of reads
in a repertoire.

\noindent\textbf{Results.}
We present \swig, a dependency-free aligner written in Rust that decides the
V, D and J alleles and their boundaries together rather than one after
another. Each way of dividing a read into segments is scored as a
log-likelihood ratio, that is, by how much better it explains the read than
random sequence does. Among the candidate alignments of a read, \swig{}
finds the best-scoring division exactly, with a dynamic-programming
recursion that tries every pair of boundary positions between neighbouring
segments. The cost of including a segment is derived from how many
alternative alleles and positions it competes with instead of being tuned
for benchmark accuracy, and alignments are scored at the mutation rate
estimated for each read. The same recursion, run forward and backward, gives
a posterior probability for every call, and the reported call is the most
probable explanation under these probabilities.

We evaluated \swig{} 0.39.7, IgBLAST 1.22 and MiXCR 4.7.0 on reads from
two simulators, GenAIRR and IgSim. GenAIRR's truth
for each segment of a read is the set of alleles that best match the read's
bases there, several when the read cannot tell them apart, and a call scores
the overlap between its alleles and that set (the Jaccard index). On 13
GenAIRR sets, two rhesus IGH panels of
50,000 reads from the IgBench harness and 11 human IGH, IGK, IGL and TRB
sets of 10,000, \swig{} had the most accurate D call on every set with a D,
the most accurate V call on every set except TRB, where it tied with
IgBLAST, and a J accuracy within 0.05 points of the best. Its D accuracy was
2.2 and 5.9 percentage points above IgBLAST's on the rhesus IgM- and
IgG-like panels and 0.7--6.0 points above on the five human IGH sets
(0--10\,\% SHM). MiXCR reports one allele per segment even when several
align equally well; credited instead with every allele tied for its best
alignment score, the more favourable scoring for MiXCR, it was 3.6 and 7.0
points below \swig{} on rhesus D and 2.3--7.8 points below on human IGH D.
IgSim, the second simulator, counts a call correct when the called allele
contains the trimmed germline sequence the read was simulated from. On its
human IGH reads \swig{} called V more accurately than both tools up to
IgG-like mutation and D on unmutated and IgG-like reads, and gave a D to 18
and 16\,\% of D-free junctions on IgM- and IgG-like reads, against 49 and
31\,\% for IgBLAST and 61 and 39\,\% for MiXCR. On eight threads \swig{}
assigned about 16,000 reads per second on the IgBench panels, about 50 times
IgBLAST's throughput and 3.3--3.6 times MiXCR's on the same cores, and on
four threads 86--153 and 8--13 times as fast on nine human GenAIRR sets.

\noindent\textbf{Availability.}
\swig{} is open source under the MIT licence at
\url{https://github.com/mashu/swig} (crate \code{engine/}); it builds with
Rust $\geq 1.89$, has no dependencies, and compiles to native code and to
WebAssembly.

\noindent\textbf{Supplementary information.}
The model with its derivations, the proof of exactness, the search, the
implementation and further results are in the Supplementary Information.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

High-throughput sequencing of B- and T-cell receptor repertoires (AIRR-seq)
turns millions of rearranged receptor transcripts into reads. Each read is a
mosaic: a V, a D (heavy chains and TRB/TRD only) and a J gene segment, trimmed
by exonucleases at the joints and separated by non-templated N and
palindromic P nucleotides, and, in B cells, overlaid with somatic
hypermutation (SHM). Germline assignment names the allele of each segment and
locates its boundaries, which fixes the junction (CDR3). Clonal grouping,
mutation analysis, lineage reconstruction and inference of novel alleles all
start from these calls, so their errors propagate.

Three properties make the assignment uncertain rather than merely
expensive. First, human IGHD genes are 11--37 nucleotides long and lose
several bases at each end; a remnant of 4--8 nucleotides is common, and
random sequence matches a few bases of some D gene by chance at many
junction positions. Second, under SHM a mismatch may be a mutation, a
sequencing error or the base that separates two alleles. Third, an N base
that happens to match the neighbouring germline can be explained by either
segment, so where V ends, where D starts and where J starts are decided
together. The task is therefore to choose the most probable explanation
among many partial ones, and to say how probable it is.

Widely used tools decide the segments in sequence.
IgBLAST~\cite{ye2013igblast} aligns the read to the V germlines first and
then searches the rest of the read for D and J. IMGT/V-QUEST~\cite{brochet2008vquest},
through IMGT/JunctionAnalysis~\cite{yousfimonod2004junction}, delimits V and
J and then compares each D with the bases between them.
MiXCR~\cite{bolotin2015mixcr} uses $k$-mer seeding and alignment for speed
and likewise aligns D only between its V and J alignments. Each accepts a D
by a fixed rule. IgBLAST 1.22 by default requires at least 5 consecutive
matching D bases. MiXCR 4.7.0 drops D alignments scoring below 25, the
score of five matched bases, and keeps further D hits only within 85\,\% of
the best. IMGT/JunctionAnalysis allows at most a set number of D mismatches,
by default 4 for IGH and 0 for TRB.
Joint models also exist: SoDA~\cite{volpe2006soda} aligns V, D and J
together by dynamic programming, and iHMMune-align~\cite{gaeta2007ihmmune}
and partis~\cite{ralph2016partis} use hidden Markov models, those of partis
with parameters inferred from the repertoire, at a much higher computational
cost. Benchmarks with an ambiguity-aware
simulator~\cite{konstantinovsky2024genairr} show that these aligners make
distinct errors, most of them in D. Deciding segments in sequence lets a
neighbour's local alignment absorb a short D, and fixed thresholds for
accepting a D trade sensitivity on short remnants against spurious D calls
on D-free junctions in ways that are hard to interpret.

\swig{} instead defines one objective, a log-likelihood ratio over the whole
read, and maximises it exactly over the segment alleles and every pair of cut
points between neighbouring segments. Its contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a \emph{placement graph} over candidate V, D and J alignments whose
    semi-Markov Viterbi recursion is exact over all grammatical paths and all
    cut pairs, in time linear in the overlaps between placements
    (\cref{sec:overview}; proof in \cref{S-sec:dag});
  \item segment priors \emph{derived} as prior odds over the hypotheses
    a segment competes with,
    D trim and N/P-length priors that re-rank D alleles without changing
    how often a typical D is called, and an \emph{open-flank} cost for a D at
    a truncated read end (\cref{S-sec:objective});
  \item alignments scored at each read's own estimated mutation rate, in
    exact integer arithmetic, with forward--backward posteriors over the same
    graph, so that the call is the most probable assignment under the
    reported posterior (\cref{S-sec:perread,S-sec:posterior});
  \item a search whose every shortcut is labelled with its guarantee (exact,
    lossless, bounded, defined, approximate or heuristic), including lossless
    sibling bounds over gene frames
    (\cref{S-sec:search}), and an inter-sequence SIMD kernel that is
    bit-identical to a scalar Gotoh across x86-64, AArch64 and WebAssembly
    (\cref{S-sec:implementation});
  \item a comparison with IgBLAST and MiXCR against ambiguity-aware GenAIRR
    truth, on rhesus IGH panels through the IgBench harness and on 11 human
    IG and TR sets, and on IgSim reads with
    paired statistics and a negative-control suite
    (\cref{sec:evaluation,sec:results}), backed by ablations of every
    modelling term, a calibration of the posteriors and an attribution of the
    remaining D errors to search or model (\cref{S-sec:supresults}).
\end{enumerate}

\Cref{sec:overview} outlines the method. The Supplementary Information gives
the model with every derivation, the exact recursion and its proof, the
plausibility constraints, the search and the implementation in full
(\cref{S-sec:model,S-sec:dag,S-sec:guards,S-sec:search,S-sec:implementation});
its sections, figures, tables and equations are numbered S1, S2, \ldots

\section{Method overview}
\label{sec:overview}

This section outlines how \swig{} assigns a read. The model, the derivation
of every prior term, the recursion with its proof, the search and the
implementation are given in full in the Supplementary Information.

\paragraph{Segments as log-odds.} Given a read and germline databases of V,
D, J and optionally C alleles, \swig{} reports the strand, the alleles of the
V, D (zero or one) and J segments with their read and germline coordinates,
hence the N/P regions and the junction, and optionally the constant region.
A segment hypothesis, an allele and a read interval, is scored by affine-gap
local alignment~\cite{smith1981,gotoh1982}. The default $+2/-3$ substitution
scores are log-likelihood ratios~\cite{altschul1991,karlin1990} of
``templated by this allele'' against ``random'' at 11.2\,\% divergence
($\lambda=0.634$ nats per score unit), so alignment scores and prior terms
add on one scale (\cref{S-sec:scores}).

\paragraph{Placement graph.} A \emph{placement} is an aligned segment
hypothesis and a \emph{path} a sequence of placements allowed by the grammar
V\,$\to$\,D$^{\{0,1\}}$\,$\to$\,J, partial paths included but never a lone D.
Between consecutive placements the earlier one exits at a cut $x$, the later
one enters at a cut $y\ge x$, and the bases between are N/P
(\cref{fig:dag}). Overlapping placements may split an overlap anywhere or
leave it to neither, and placements are ordered by segment kind rather than
by read start, so a D may precede a J whose alignment reaches back over it:
the configuration in which a sequential annotator lets the J ``eat'' the D.

\paragraph{Objective.} Read positions run from 0 to $|q|$. A path is a
sequence
\begin{equation*}
  \pi=\bigl((p_1,y_1,x_1),\dots,(p_n,y_n,x_n)\bigr)
\end{equation*}
of placements $p_i$, each of kind $k(p_i)\in\{\mathrm V,\mathrm D,\mathrm J\}$,
entered at read position $y_i$ and left at $x_i$, whose kinds spell V, J, VJ,
VD, DJ or VDJ, with $y_1=\operatorname{start}(p_1)$,
$x_n=\operatorname{end}(p_n)$ and $x_i\le y_{i+1}$; cuts fall on aligned
bases, and the full conditions are in \cref{S-sec:objective}. Its value, in
nats, is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  v(\pi) \;&=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} w(p_i,y_i,x_i)
     \;-\; c_{\text{start}}(p_1) \;-\; c_{\text{end}}(p_n),\\
  w(p,y,x) \;&=\; W_p(y,x) \;-\; C_{k(p)}
     \;+\; \ind[k(p)=\mathrm D]\,\bigl(\nu\,(x-y) - \tau_5\,t_5(p,y) - \tau_3\,t_3(p,x) + \kappa\bigr),\\
  c_{\text{start}}(p) \;&=\; \ind[k(p)=\mathrm D]\;\copen{V}\bigl(\operatorname{start}(p)\bigr),
  \qquad
  c_{\text{end}}(p) \;=\; \ind[k(p)=\mathrm D]\;\copen{J}\bigl(|q|-\operatorname{end}(p)\bigr),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
where $\ind[\cdot]$ is 1 when its condition holds and 0 otherwise.
$W_p(y,x)$ is the log-likelihood ratio of the alignment columns that $p$
keeps between read positions $y$ and $x$: $\lambda$ times their alignment
score at the fixed scale, and by default their worth at the read's own
divergence (below). Every other term is a log prior odds or log-likelihood
ratio with a stated derivation (\cref{S-sec:objective}). The presence cost
$C_k$ of a segment of kind $k$ follows from the number of gene-and-position
hypotheses the segment competes with, $P^{*}_k$ score units: for the IMGT
human IGH set $\pD=10.45$, so an exact 5-mer D (score 10) loses and an exact
6-mer (12) wins, without a threshold tuned for benchmark accuracy. For a D,
geometric trim priors charge $\tau_5$ and $\tau_3$ for each of the
$t_5(p,y)$ and $t_3(p,x)$ germline bases its clip leaves out at the 5$'$ and
3$'$ ends of the allele, an N/P-length prior credits $\nu$ for each of the
$x-y$ read bases it keeps, and the credit $\kappa$ keeps a D with the prior's
mean trims as likely to be called as before. A D that starts or ends the
path pays the open-flank cost $\copen{V}$ or $\copen{J}$ of the read bases
between it and the read end, the log-likelihood ratio that those bases would
have shown the missing V or J. Ties go to fewer
Ds and then to more germline support, and co-optimal alleles are all
reported. A read is called only when its path holds a V or J anchor at
$E\le10^{-3}$ (\cref{S-sec:significance}).

\paragraph{Exact adjudication.} Every term of \cref{eq:objective} depends on
at most one cut, so the best pair of cuts on an edge of the graph factorises
into two running maxima. A semi-Markov Viterbi
recursion~\cite{viterbi1967,yu2010hsmm} over the placements therefore returns
the exact optimum over all grammatical paths, alleles and cut pairs, in time
linear in the overlaps between placements (\cref{S-thm:dag,S-alg:dag}). The
test suite checks it against exhaustive enumeration of every path and cut
pair, including on constructed reads that restricted searches get wrong.

\paragraph{Per-read divergence and posteriors.} The $+2/-3$ scores are
log-odds only at 11.2\,\% divergence, which makes unmutated reads
under-confident and heavily mutated reads over-confident. \swig{} therefore
estimates each read's divergence from the V and J columns no path contests
and values every column at it, in exact integer arithmetic
(\cref{S-sec:perread}). Forward--backward over the same graph gives the
posterior of every placement and of D presence; the call is a mode of the
reported distribution (\cref{S-sec:posterior}).

\paragraph{Plausibility constraints.} On truncated, chimeric and cross-locus
reads the optimum need not pair a V and a J that can form one rearrangement.
Minimum hit lengths, a V--J pairing rule and a span limit are applied outside
the objective; when the optimum breaks them the call is the best admissible
subset optimum (\cref{S-sec:guards}). Junctions are reported only from
conserved anchors (Cys104, Trp/Phe118) that the called alignments align.

\paragraph{Search and implementation.} \Cref{fig:pipeline} shows the
pipeline of one read. V candidates are retrieved by $k$-mer votes over gene
frames, aligned in a band around the seed diagonals and ranked with lossless
sibling bounds; J is aligned to every allele of the V's locus; D is searched
in junction windows behind a lossless $k$-mer prefilter. Each step is
labelled with what it can lose: exact, lossless, bounded, defined,
approximate or heuristic (\cref{S-tab:search}). Alignments run on an
inter-sequence SIMD Gotoh kernel~\cite{rognes2011} (AVX-512BW, AVX2, NEON,
WebAssembly \code{simd128} or portable lanes) whose scores and tracebacks are
bit-identical to a scalar Gotoh, so the output is byte-identical across
backends, thread counts and platforms (\cref{S-sec:implementation}). \swig{}
reads FASTA, FASTQ or AIRR tables and writes the AIRR Rearrangement
schema~\cite{vanderheiden2018airr} with posteriors, from a command line, a
Rust library or a WebAssembly module.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

All reads in this paper are simulated, so that every call can be scored
against a known answer; none of the results is external biological
validation (\cref{sec:discussion}). Two independent simulators are used, and
\swig{}, IgBLAST 1.22 and MiXCR 4.7.0 are compared on the reads of both. The
numbers in \cref{sec:results} and in the Supplementary Information were
produced with \swig{} 0.39.7 for this paper. The rhesus panels of
\cref{sec:genairr} are the IgBench run described below; its human sets are
the GenAIRR grid behind the D-prior design analysis (\cref{S-sec:dpriors}),
to which MiXCR 4.7.0 was added for this paper.

\paragraph{GenAIRR.} We use reads simulated by
GenAIRR 2.3.1~\cite{konstantinovsky2024genairr} in two settings. Through the
IgBench harness (\url{https://github.com/mashu/IgBench.jl}) we use the
bundled \code{rhesus\_igh} experiment (rhesus macaque IGH alleles): two
panels of 50,000 reads with IgM-like and IgG-like S5F~\cite{yaari2013s5f}
SHM rates, polymerase indel probability 0.003, and a secreted constant
region placed on the simulated sequence. With GenAIRR's built-in human
references (IGH from OGRDB~\cite{lees2020ogrdb}: 198 V, 33 D and 7 J
alleles; IGK 168 V and 8 J; IGL 181 V and 10 J; TRB 98 V, 3 D and 16 J) we
simulate 11 sets of 10,000 reads (seed 7, forward strand): unmutated; S5F
mutation at 2, 5 and 10\,\%; and \emph{realistic}: S5F at 5\,\% with a
5$'$ primer trim of 0--15 and a 3$'$ trim of 0--5 bases, 0--1 polymerase
indels, 0.2\,\% PCR and 0.2\,\% sequencing errors and 0--2 N calls (for
TRB, the same artefacts without SHM). The D trim and N/P-length priors were
designed on the IGH sets of this grid, so four IGH scenarios were simulated
again from a disjoint part of GenAIRR's random stream (seed 50000) as an
out-of-sample check (\cref{S-sec:dpriors}). In both settings every tool
assigns against the same GenAIRR-exported V, D and J FASTA. GenAIRR's truth
for each segment is a \emph{tie set}: each allele earns a point for every
read base that matches it at the same germline index as the sampled allele,
over the segment's true read region, and every allele at the maximum is in
the set. A call $P$ against truth $G$ scores the Jaccard index
$|P\cap G|/|P\cup G|$ per read and segment (both empty score 1, one empty
0), IgBench's allele accuracy, and tables give the mean in \%. One correct
allele against a two-allele tie therefore scores $\tfrac12$, as does a
two-allele call against a one-allele truth.

\paragraph{IgSim.} \swig{} ships a dependency-free Rust port of the
generative model and presets of IgSim.jl~\cite{igsimjl}: uniform allele
choice, exonuclease trims, N additions, random flanks, gated SHM with
single-base indels, and instrument substitutions, with every read base
labelled by its germline origin. The presets are \emph{easy} (no SHM),
\emph{mid} (IgM-like: SHM on 40\,\% of reads at 0.5--7\,\%; 1-nt indels
switched on for 8\,\% of reads at up to 0.2\,\% per base, so about 2\,\% carry
one) and \emph{hard} (IgG-like: SHM on 97\,\% of reads, median about 7\,\%;
indels on 15\,\% at up to 0.4\,\% per base, about 7\,\% carry one); we also run
the \emph{hard} preset with the SHM rate fixed at 30\,\% on every read to
probe the extreme.
The germline is the human IGH set of IMGT/GENE-DB~\cite{giudicelli2005genedb}
release 202632-7 (480 V, 48 D and 15 J alleles, of which \swig{} merges
identical sequences to 457, 41 and 15), given to every tool as plain FASTA:
\swig{} annotates the anchors itself. The simulator draws only V and J alleles
with a trusted anchor, since a junction truth built on a guessed anchor would
rest on the annotation it is meant to test. An allele call is correct when it
is \emph{germline-equivalent} to the truth: the called allele contains the
trimmed, pre-mutation germline that was simulated, so no method could
separate the two from the read. Junctions are correct when they equal the
true Cys104--Trp/Phe118 sequence; junction accuracy is over the reads whose
two anchor codons are templated in the read (9,953, 8,130 and 4,667 of
10,000 on \emph{easy}, \emph{mid} and \emph{hard}; 1,441 of 3,000 at 30\,\%
SHM). Because the trade-off between finding short
D remnants and leaving D-free junctions without a D can be moved at will, D
accuracy is always reported together with the share of D-free reads that are
given a D, and by remnant length.

\paragraph{Comparisons and statistics.} Two callers or settings are compared
on identical reads. For each metric \code{swig evaluate}, which scores any
AIRR Rearrangement table and so serves every tool alike, computes the
accuracy difference with its 95\,\% paired Wald interval and an exact
two-sided McNemar test~\cite{mcnemar1947} on the reads only one of them gets
right; we report the differences and the McNemar $p$-values.
 Posteriors are scored by reliability bins, the expected
calibration error~\cite{naeini2015ece}
$\text{ECE}=\sum_b n_b\,|\bar p_b-\bar e_b|/n$ and the Brier
score~\cite{brier1950}.

\paragraph{Tools and hardware.} On the IgBench panels IgBLAST 1.22.0 was
run with \code{-organism rhesus\_monkey}, \code{-ig\_seqtype Ig},
\code{-outfmt 19}, the GenAIRR J auxiliary file and a rhesus IGHC region
database; on the human GenAIRR sets with \code{-organism human},
\code{-ig\_seqtype Ig} (\code{TCR} for TRB) and \code{-outfmt 19}, on
\code{makeblastdb} databases of the same FASTA with a J auxiliary file; for
IGK and IGL, which have no D, it was given the IGH D database, which
\code{igblastn} requires but does not use there.
MiXCR 4.7.0 was run as IgBench runs it: \code{buildLibrary} on the GenAIRR
FASTAs, then \code{align} with the \code{generic-amplicon} preset and
floating alignment boundaries (at the V start, and at the C, or on the human
reads, which end in J, at the J end), then \code{exportAirr}; it requires a
Mi Laboratory licence (\code{MI\_LICENSE} or a \code{mi.license} file). Its
AIRR call names a single allele per segment, which the Jaccard score cannot
credit fully when the truth is a tie, so we also export every hit with its
score (\code{exportAlignments}) and report MiXCR counting every hit tied with
its top score (\emph{tied hits}), the fairer comparison with IgBLAST and
\swig{}, which list co-optimal alleles; IgBench's own MiXCR result is the AIRR
call. \swig{} 0.39.7 ran with its default (R-optimized) calling profile, from
a prepared reference on the IgBench panels and from FASTA with each allele's
anchor in its header on the human sets. On IgSim
comparisons IgBLAST used \code{-organism human} with custom IMGT databases;
its auxiliary file lacked IGHJ5*03 and IGHJ5*04, which we added with the
frame and CDR3 end of IGHJ5*01 (matching \swig{}'s anchors). MiXCR ran with
the options of the human GenAIRR sets from a \code{buildLibrary} library of
the same IMGT FASTA. \code{buildLibrary} drops any sequence with a wildcard
base, so in MiXCR's copy the 1--4 unsequenced N bases of six V alleles, the
true V of 1.3--1.5\,\% of reads, were filled from the closest allele, and
MiXCR could call every allele. Germline-equivalent scoring already credits
an allele that the read cannot tell apart from the truth, so on IgSim MiXCR
is scored by its AIRR call.

Every run, for accuracy and for speed, used one machine: an Intel Core
i9-13980HX laptop (eight performance cores with two hardware threads each and
16 efficiency cores; AVX2, no AVX-512; 62\,GB of memory; Linux, on mains
power). Timed runs were made one at a time, with the tool's own thread
option set to 1, 4 or 8 and every command restricted to the performance
cores, so that the three tools ran on the same cores: on the IgBench panels
and the human GenAIRR sets they shared the 16 hardware threads of the eight
performance cores, and for the IgSim timings of \cref{tab:speed} each thread
had a performance core of its own. The machine ran a desktop session, and
timed commands had network access, so their times include IgBLAST's usage
report to NCBI and MiXCR's online licence check. All timings are wall-clock
times of the whole command (IgBLAST: \code{igblastn}; MiXCR: \code{align},
Java start-up included; \swig{}: the CLI, reference preparation included)
unless stated. IgBench GenAIRR runs used eight threads, the human GenAIRR
sets four.

\subsection{Germline-equivalent accuracy, D-free junctions and junctions}
\label{sec:igsim}

\Cref{tab:igsim} compares \swig, IgBLAST and MiXCR on IgSim reads against
the IMGT human IGH set, read by read. \swig{} calls the V allele correctly
more often than both on unmutated, IgM-like and IgG-like reads ($p<10^{-6}$
for each comparison). It calls the D allele correctly more often on
unmutated and IgG-like reads ($+2.2$ points over IgBLAST and $+4.7$ over
MiXCR on \emph{hard}, $p<10^{-12}$), and the J allele on IgG-like reads. It
also gives a D to far fewer D-free junctions (18.4 against 49.4 and
61.4\,\% on \emph{mid}, 15.8 against 30.8 and 39.3\,\% on \emph{hard}). On
remnants of 8 or more nucleotides \swig's D is better than both on every
preset.

MiXCR's D is below both other tools on every preset (98.45, 91.42 and
82.25\,\% on \emph{easy}, \emph{mid} and \emph{hard}). Its V allele
accuracy is level with IgBLAST's up to IgM-like mutation (99.51 and 98.17
against 99.47 and 98.06\,\%; $p\ge0.14$) and below both on IgG-like reads,
where it leaves 167 reads (1.7\,\%) unassigned. At 30\,\% SHM it assigns
only 900 of 3,000 reads. That is also why it gives a D to the fewest D-free
junctions there (25.6\,\%): an unassigned read receives no D.

IgBLAST's 0.5-point higher D accuracy on \emph{mid}
($p=0.017$) comes entirely from remnants of 1--3 nucleotides (74.3 against
36.6\,\%), which no method can identify: most D alleles contain so short a
remnant and are therefore germline-equivalent to it (on \emph{mid}, 36 of the
48 IMGT D names on average), so a tool that always calls a D is mostly
credited there, and pays for it on D-free junctions.

IgBLAST reports more exact junctions (99.94 against 99.53\,\% on \emph{mid},
98.33 against 96.81\,\% on \emph{hard}); MiXCR's are 99.72 and 96.29\,\%
exact, between the two on \emph{mid} and below both on \emph{hard}. This is
the cost of \swig's
plausibility constraints, which report a junction only from conserved
anchors that the called V and J alignments actually align: without them
\swig's junctions are 99.98 and 99.66\,\% exact (\cref{S-tab:ablation}), above
IgBLAST's. The constraints in turn withhold junctions from reads whose
anchor codon is not templated in the read (\emph{hard}: 20.9\,\% of such
reads receive one, against 98.0\,\% without the constraints and 98.5\,\% for
IgBLAST).

At a fixed 30\,\% SHM, beyond IgSim's IgG-like regime, the picture is mixed.
\swig{} keeps a large lead over IgBLAST on D ($+7.3$ points) and J
($+13.6$), but IgBLAST is better on V allele ($+3.4$) and V gene (95.0
against 89.7\,\%) and on junctions. MiXCR, which assigns 30\,\% of these
reads, is below both on every segment. \swig{} leaves 4.4\,\% of these reads uncalled (133 of 3,000),
106 of them (3.5\,\% of all reads) because its strand test chose the wrong
strand, as the strand test and seed
retrieval run out of intact $k$-mers (\cref{sec:discussion}); every read it
calls is correctly oriented.

\subsection{Specificity}
\label{sec:specificity}

\swig{} made no call on 2,000 random reads with the length distribution of
the \emph{mid} set, and none on 1,000 IGK reads assigned against the IGH
database: the anchor gate requires a V or J hit at $E\le10^{-3}$. IgBLAST,
whose default E-value threshold for saving hits is a permissive 20, gave a V to
1,272 random reads (and a junction to 93) and an IGHV to 997 of the IGK
reads. Filtering its V hits at $E\le10^{-3}$ removes every random-read call
but keeps an IGHV on 470 of the 1,000 IGK reads. MiXCR, like \swig, made no
call on either set.

\subsection{Speed}
\label{sec:speed}

On the IgBench GenAIRR panels (\cref{fig:igbench-timing}; eight threads,
scored annotate pass only) \swig{} assigned 16,200--16,500 reads per second,
3.3--3.6 times MiXCR's 4,600--4,900 and about 50 times IgBLAST's 320--330.
On nine of the human GenAIRR sets (\cref{S-tab:genairr-speed}; four threads,
one run each) it assigned 15,400--28,500 reads per second, 86--153 times
IgBLAST's throughput and 8.3--13.4 times MiXCR's. \Cref{tab:speed} gives
provisional throughput of the three tools on the 10,000-read IgSim sets on
one, four and eight threads, as wall clock of the whole command; \swig's
includes database preparation (25--28\,ms for the IMGT IGH set, every allele
annotated on load). Each command also has a fixed cost: a one-read run took
0.05\,s for \swig, 0.4--1.5\,s for IgBLAST (0.05\,s with its usage report
switched off) and 1.7--4.6\,s for MiXCR across our timing passes. On four
threads \swig{} assigns 11,100--11,700 reads per second with posteriors, 82
times IgBLAST's throughput and 12--13 times MiXCR's on the same reads and
cores, and 14,400--14,800 without them; posteriors add 18--33\,\% to the run
time. From one thread to eight, \swig's throughput grows 5.6--6.1-fold,
IgBLAST's 7.4--7.7-fold and MiXCR's 3.7--5.1-fold. On one thread the default
AVX2 kernel gives 2.3--2.4 times the throughput of the scalar kernel, and
every SIMD backend and thread count produces byte-identical output
(\cref{S-sec:kernels}).

\subsection{Model diagnostics}
\label{sec:diagnostics}

The Supplementary Information tests each modelling term on its own; we
summarise it here. Switching the D trim and N/P priors off costs 0.37 and
1.08 points of D accuracy on \emph{mid} and \emph{hard}
(\cref{S-tab:ablation}) and 0.3--3.1 points of IGH D on the human GenAIRR
sets (\cref{S-fig:dpriors}). On reads that stop at their D or start in CDR3 the
open-flank cost keeps the right D on 82--94\,\% of reads, while a V followed
by 100 random bases receives a D on 2.6 and 11.2\,\% of reads, against 88
and 93\,\% without the cost and the plausibility constraints
(\cref{S-tab:truncated}). With a D remnant of at least 8 nucleotides the true
D is almost never outside the search windows (at most 0.04\,\% of reads) or
covered by the called J (at most 0.14\,\%); the remaining D errors are
choices between similar alleles on short, mutated remnants
(\cref{S-tab:derrors}). The posteriors are well calibrated up to IgG-like
mutation, with an expected calibration error of at most 0.5\,\% for V,
0.8\,\% for J, 1.9\,\% for the D allele and 1.1\,\% for D presence; at
30\,\% SHM the V and J posteriors are over-confident
(\cref{S-tab:calibration,S-fig:calibration}). With a C database the primary
C call is right on at least 98.5\,\% of reads that show 16 or more
nucleotides of C (\cref{S-sec:constant-results}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\swig{} replaces the sequence of local decisions of a conventional
annotator with one objective and its exact maximiser. Three consequences
stand out in the results. First, the failure mode that motivates joint
placement, a neighbouring segment absorbing a short D, has essentially
disappeared (\cref{S-tab:derrors}); the D errors that remain are choices
between similar alleles on short, mutated remnants, where the evidence is
present but weak. That is a modelling question, and the D trim and N/P
priors show that modelling, not search, is where further accuracy lies:
moving two cut-local prior terms into the graph raised human IGH D accuracy
on GenAIRR reads by up to 3.1 points at no measurable cost in speed
(\cref{S-fig:dpriors}). Second,
because every non-alignment term is a log prior odds with a stated
derivation, the trade-off between finding short D remnants and leaving D-free
junctions alone is moved by an interpretable prior rather than by ad hoc
score thresholds: its user-facing knob is the prior odds of a D
(\cref{fig:operating}). The default operating point also depends on the
credit $\kappa$, chosen on simulated reads, and on the 4-base D admission
run. Third, the posterior
is a by-product of the same recursion and the call is its mode, so the
reported probabilities describe the call that is reported; they are well
calibrated up to IgG-like mutation levels.

The pair of simulators matters. GenAIRR (via IgBench) scores calls against
tie sets that count only the bases a read actually shows, which rewards
reporting genuine ties and penalises over-reporting; IgSim scores germline
equivalence and separates D accuracy from D-free specificity and remnant
length. On both, the IgBLAST comparison favours \swig{} on V and D up to
IgG-like mutation, except D on IgSim IgM-like reads, where IgBLAST's lead
comes from 1--3-nt remnants. MiXCR 4.7.0, the faster of the two established
tools here, trails \swig{} on V and D on every set of both simulators. On
GenAIRR, counted by its tied hits, which a tie-set truth needs for a fair
comparison with tools that report co-optimal alleles, its D gap is
2.3--7.8 points on human IGH and 3.6--7.0 on the rhesus panels; its single
AIRR call widens it to 10.8--17.5. On IgSim its D gap is 1.4--4.7 points up
to IgG-like mutation, and on IgM- and IgG-like reads it gives a D to more
D-free junctions than either other tool. \swig{} is also faster: 3.3--3.6
times on the IgBench panels and 8--16 times on the 10,000-read sets of both
simulators.
On IgSim, IgBLAST reports more exact junctions because \swig{} withholds a
junction whose anchor codon its alignments do not reach, and at 30\,\% SHM
IgBLAST's V calls are better.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{All results are on simulated reads.} Both simulators make
    simplifying assumptions: uniform allele usage, independent trims, no
    chimeras or long adapters, and in IgSim no hotspot-targeted SHM and no
    codon-length indels. Several design parameters were measured on
    simulated reads: the open-flank allowance from simulated junction
    lengths, the D-cost credit on IgSim, and the D trim and N/P priors from
    an analysis of the previous release's losses on the human GenAIRR design
    grid of \cref{S-fig:dpriors}. The priors' means (5, 7 and 8 bases) are
    generic rather than fitted, and GenAIRR's own trim tables did no better
    in the prototype; on human GenAIRR reads disjoint from the design sets
    the priors gained as much (\cref{S-tab:oos}), but both come from one
    simulator. The IgBench head-to-head (\cref{tab:genairr}) uses a
    different species and SHM schedule. Validation on real repertoires, for
    example donors with sequenced germline genotypes, is the most important
    next step.
  \item \emph{Comparisons.} We did not compare with partis or IMGT/V-QUEST.
    MiXCR's tied hits are derived from the hit scores it exports, not an
    output mode of MiXCR itself, whose AIRR export names one allele per
    segment. On IgSim, MiXCR's copy of six IMGT V alleles has its N bases
    filled in, because its library builder rejects them. Throughput was
    measured on one laptop running a desktop session, with network access.
    The IgSim timings of \cref{tab:speed} are provisional: in a second pass
    with the cores shared, the same commands' times differed by up to 7\,\%
    for \swig{} and IgBLAST and 24\,\% for MiXCR, and on a slow connection
    MiXCR's network calls held some runs at 74\,s whatever their thread count
    (those runs were discarded). Other hardware may rank the tools' thread
    scaling differently.
  \item \emph{The plausibility constraints are inherited thresholds}, not
    terms of the likelihood, and the call under them is the best admissible
    subset optimum rather than an exact maximum over every admissible path.
    They cost 0.4--2.9 points of junction exactness on simulated reads
    (\cref{S-tab:ablation}) in exchange for robustness on truncated, chimeric
    and cross-locus reads that the simulators do not produce. A model of
    truncation and template switching would price what they forbid.
  \item \emph{D on D-free junctions.} 16--18\,\% of IgSim reads without a D
    remnant still receive one, and a chance D within about 30 bases of a
    truncated read's V is indistinguishable from a real D there
    (\cref{S-tab:truncated}). On TRB, whose two D genes are short and rarely
    trimmed as much as the prior expects, the D-cost credit gives D-free
    reads a D more often.
  \item \emph{Extreme mutation.} At 30\,\% SHM the strand test and seed
    retrieval lose intact $k$-mers: 4.4\,\% of reads are left uncalled, most of
    them (3.5\,\% of all reads) because the wrong strand was chosen, V accuracy falls below IgBLAST's, and
    the V and J posteriors are
    over-confident because the true gene is no longer among the candidates.
  \item \emph{Statistics.} E-values use exact ungapped $\lambda$ and $K$
    (anti-conservative for gapped scores) and the total database length
    (conservative for near-identical alleles); they gate hits and are not
    calibrated $p$-values. One divergence per read assumes uniform mutation
    across V, D and J.
  \item \emph{One alignment per D allele and window.} A D allele's optimal
    local alignment in its window need not be its best clip at the chosen
    cuts; this is the one remaining approximation of the segmentation model.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future work}

The D-presence residual of the posterior tracks junction length, which
points to an N/P length prior that decides D presence and not only re-ranks
Ds. Allele usage is uniform in the model; usage priors learned from the
repertoire itself, iterated with the calls, would matter where one gene
dominates. Gapped Karlin--Altschul parameters and an effective database size
can be fitted by simulation. Above all, the model's assumptions need testing
on real data.

\section{Conclusion}

\swig{} shows that germline assignment can be posed as one likelihood ratio
and solved exactly, fast enough for whole repertoires: an exact semi-Markov
recursion over every pair of cuts between neighbouring segments, priors set
by the number of hypotheses each segment competes with, alignments scored at
each read's own mutation rate and posteriors from the same recursion.
Every search shortcut carries a stated guarantee, and the remaining errors
are located by construction. On GenAIRR reads it has the best V and D of
\swig{}, IgBLAST 1.22 and MiXCR 4.7.0 on every rhesus and human set (V level
with IgBLAST on TRB), and the best J on every human IG set and the rhesus IgG
panel, also on human IGH reads disjoint from those its D priors were designed
on; on IgSim reads it leads IgBLAST and MiXCR on V and D up to IgG-like
mutation (except IgBLAST on IgM-like D, whose lead comes from unidentifiable
1--3-nt remnants) and trails IgBLAST on junction exactness and on V at
30\,\% SHM. On the same cores it ran about 50--150 times as fast as IgBLAST
and 3--16 times as fast as MiXCR.

\section*{Availability}

\swig{} 0.39.7 is free software under the MIT licence, available at
\url{https://github.com/mashu/swig} (Rust crate in \code{engine/}). It builds
