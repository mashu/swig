Goal of the session: by the end, everyone should be able to explain what an alignment score means, why the matrix is filled the way it is, what makes Swig fast, and why placing V, D and J jointly matters. The read in the title is a real slice of the example we use later.
The question

Every receptor read is a mosaic

A developing B cell cuts and joins one V, one D and one J gene. The joints are trimmed by exonucleases and filled with random N nucleotides. Later, somatic hypermutation sprinkles point mutations on top.

An annotator must answer

  • Which V, D and J alleles made this read?
  • Where does each segment start and stop, which bases are N/P, and what is the junction (CDR3)?

The question

Three things make the answer uncertain

Short D

The D is tiny

Human IGHD genes are 11–37 nt. After trimming, a remnant of 4–8 nt is common. Random sequence matches 5 nt of some D by chance all the time.

Mutation

Mutations look like alleles

Under hypermutation (IgG reads carry about 7 % mutated bases) a mismatch may be a mutation, a sequencing error, or the base that separates two alleles.

Boundaries

Junctions are ambiguous

An N base that happens to match the V can be explained by either. Where V stops, where D starts and where J starts must be decided together.

So the job is not "find a match". It is choosing the most probable explanation among many partial ones. Everything in this course is a tool for that choice.

D lengths: human IGHD genes range roughly from 11 nt (IGHD7-27) to 37 nt (IGHD3-16). In the simulator's mid preset the D is trimmed by geometric amounts on both ends.
Alignment from first principles

An alignment is a hypothesis about history

Write two sequences one above the other. Each column claims something happened: the base was copied (match), changed (mismatch), or one side gained or lost bases (gap).

Gap –

An insertion or deletion (indel). Rare in receptors, so it should cost more than a mismatch.

The score of an alignment adds up one number per column. The best alignment is the one with the highest score. Two questions follow: which numbers, and how to find the best one without trying every alignment.

There are astronomically many ways to place gaps. For two 300-nt sequences the number of alignments exceeds 10^170. We need dynamic programming.
Alignment from first principles

Rows are one sequence, columns the other

Put the read down the rows and the germline across the columns. A cell (i, j) asks: "what is the best alignment that ends with read base i and germline base j?"

An alignment is a path through this grid:

  • ↘ diagonal: both advance, a match or mismatch column.
  • → right: germline advances alone, a deletion in the read.
  • ↓ down: read advances alone, an insertion in the read.

Swig's own code uses exactly this layout: rows are query positions, columns reference positions, and a diagonal is d = i − j.

Hover any cell: it highlights which read base is compared with which germline base. The two buttons show two different paths, which are two different alignments of the same pair.
Which numbers? Log-odds

A score is a log-odds ratio

For each pair of letters a, b compare two stories:

s(a,b) = log [ qab / (pa · pb) ]
  • qab: how often a and b are aligned in truly related sequences.
  • papb: how often they would meet by chance.

Positive means "more often than chance". Because logs add, the sum over columns is the log-likelihood ratio of the whole alignment: related versus random.

BLOSUM62 (Henikoff & Henikoff 1992) counts substitutions in conserved protein blocks, clustered at 62 % identity, and stores the log-odds in half-bits: s = 2·log₂(ratio).

W–W scores 11: tryptophan is aligned with itself 25.5 ≈ 45 times more often than chance predicts. Rare and important residues are rarely replaced.
BLOSUM is the classic teaching example of log-odds. I versus L is +2 (both hydrophobic), D versus E is +2 (both acidic), W versus G is negative. Swig aligns nucleotides, so it uses the DNA version of the same idea, next slide.
Which numbers? The DNA version in Swig

+2 / −3 is a log-odds matrix for DNA

Swig aligns nucleotides, so its "BLOSUM" is a 4×4 matrix: match +2, mismatch −3, an N scores 0. These are not arbitrary. With the Karlin–Altschul constant λ = 0.634, they are exactly the log-odds of "copied from this gene at 11.2 % divergence" against "random sequence".

match   : ln 4(1−p)
mismatch: ln (4p/3)

Novelty: each read gets its own p. Swig estimates the read's divergence p̂ from V and J columns that no boundary decision touches, then scores the call at that divergence. An unmutated read makes a single mismatch strong evidence; a heavily mutated read makes it cheap.

Example from the docs: a single nucleotide difference between two alleles is worth ln(3(1−p)/p) = 8.0 nats on an unmutated read at p = 0.1 %, not the 3.2 nats a fixed +2/−3 scale gives. That is why allele calls on clean reads become far more confident.
Which numbers? Gaps

Affine gaps: opening costs more than extending

An indel is one rare event, whether it removes 1 base or 3. A linear penalty charges every gap base the same, so one 3-base deletion would look like three independent events.

gap(L) = open + (L − 1) · extend

Swig's V scoring is open = −9, extend = −1: a 3-nt deletion costs −11, barely more than a 1-nt one (−9). In hypermutated antibodies indels are rare and often a whole codon, so this is the right shape.

Defaults: V (2, −3, −9, −1); D (2, −3, −13, −1); J (2, −3, −17, −2).

affinelinear
Note the constructor in scoring.rs refuses open cheaper than extend: otherwise the optimum would price a long gap as repeated openings.
How to find the best path

Dynamic programming: each cell from three neighbours

Start in Global mode, press Step a few times, then click any filled cell to see which neighbour it came from. Then switch to Local: negative cells are floored at 0, and the best cell (orange ring) can be anywhere. Gotoh adds the gap-state bookkeeping for affine gaps; click a cell to see its three values.
How to find the best path

Three ideas, often confused

AlgorithmWhat it addsRecurrence changeAnswers
Needleman–Wunsch (1970)The DP grid itselfH = max(diag + s, up + g, left + g)Best global alignment, end to end
Smith–Waterman (1981)A floor at zeroH = max(0, diag + s, up + g, left + g)Best local alignment: the best-matching piece of each
Gotoh (1982)Affine gaps at the same costE, F track "already in a gap"; extend is cheapSame answers, with open + extend gaps, still O(n·m)
Why local? A read holds V, then junction, then J, plus primers and adapters. A germline V only explains part of the read, and the read only covers part of a J. Local alignment lets each gene explain only the piece it fits.
Why Gotoh? Done naively, affine gaps make every cell look back over all gap lengths: O(n·m·(n+m)). Gotoh's two extra matrices remember whether we are already inside a gap, which brings it back to O(n·m). Swig runs "Smith–Waterman with Gotoh gaps".
Smith–Waterman and Gotoh answer different questions: SW is about local versus global, Gotoh is about how gaps are charged. They combine. The scalar kernel in Swig is tested to equal a textbook Gotoh exactly.
How to find the best path, faster

Banded alignment: compute only near the diagonal

If we already know roughly where the read sits on the gene (from seeds, next section), the optimal path stays close to one diagonal d = i − j. We only fill cells with d in [lo, hi].

Work drops from n·m to about n·(band width). Memory too: one rolling row plus traceback for band cells only.

The risk: an indel shifts the path to another diagonal. An indel bigger than the band escapes it.

Swig's V band is the diagonal range of the seed cluster ± 8. When seeds on both sides of a long indel form two clusters, the band spans both. J alleles are short and aligned without a band; D windows are small.

A 300-nt V against a 400-nt read: full matrix 120,000 cells, a band of 17 diagonals about 5,000. The canvas is a 120 by 80 cartoon of that.
Speed tricks · k-mers

A k-mer is a word of k letters, stored as a number

Slide a window of k bases along a sequence: every position gives one k-mer. Two related sequences share many identical k-mers; unrelated ones share few.

Trick 1, 2 bits per base. A=00, C=01, G=10, T=11. A 9-mer is an 18-bit integer, so it can be used directly as an array index: no hashing, no string comparison.

Trick 2, rolling update. Moving the window by one base costs one shift, one OR and one AND:

key = ((key << 2) | code(base)) & (4k − 1)

Swig uses 9-mers for V seeds (7-mers as a fallback for heavily mutated reads), 11-mers to decide the read's strand, and 4-mers to prefilter D alleles.

4^9 = 262,144 possible 9-mers, so a direct-address table of offsets fits easily in memory.
Speed tricks · counting k-mers

The germline index: one array lookup per k-mer

Built once per germline set, before any read. For every k-mer key, the offsets array says where its occurrences start in one flat postings array (compressed sparse rows).

Lookup = two loads. postings[offsets[key] .. offsets[key+1]] is every place the k-mer occurs.

Packed postings. One 32-bit word holds both the gene frame (20 bits) and the position (12 bits): germline genes are under 4,096 nt.

No hash map for votes either. Each vote is packed into one 64-bit number and the votes are radix-sorted in linear time, then one scan groups them by gene and diagonal.

Seeds that occur too often (low-complexity) are skipped, and a seed occurring twice in one gene does not vote, so a repeat like AGTAGTAGT cannot collect quadratic votes.

Why not an FM-index? Germline databases are a few hundred kilobases and seeds have fixed length, so a flat k-mer array is smaller in constants and faster than backward search.
Speed tricks · seeding

Shared k-mers vote for a diagonal

Each k-mer shared by the read (rows) and a germline (columns) is a dot. If the read came from this gene, the dots line up on one diagonal d = i − p. The histogram on the right counts votes per diagonal.

  • A mutation destroys the k overlapping k-mers. Longer k is more specific but more fragile.
  • A deletion shifts later seeds to another diagonal: two clusters, which Swig joins when they are close.
  • 9-mers vote with weight 4; while the leader has fewer than 25 nine-mer seeds, 7-mers join with weight 1.
Push the mutation rate to 20-30 % with k = 11: the diagonal fades. Drop k to 7 and it returns, with more random dots. This is why Swig keeps 9-mers as primary seeds and adds 7-mers only for weakly supported reads.
Speed tricks · fewer, smarter alignments

Rank genes, then align as few alleles as possible

Gene frames

Alleles share coordinates

Alleles of one gene that differ by SNPs or truncation are placed in one coordinate frame. A seed shared by all alleles is one posting and one vote. Retrieval work scales with genes, not alleles.

Noise-aware budget

How many genes to keep

Keep 3 genes, and up to 16 while their votes are within two standard deviations of the leader's: z·√(2·4·L). Mutated reads with few votes get a wider net automatically.

Lossless bounds

Skip what cannot win

Align one representative per frame. For each sibling allele compute an upper bound from the positions where it differs. If the bound is below the current 3rd-best score, the allele is skipped. The ranking is provably the same as aligning everything.

D: every allele sharing a 4-mer with the junction window is aligned, unbanded. J: every allele, unbanded, downstream of the V.
Strand: decided once, by counting read positions whose 11-mer occurs in the germline on each strand against a Poisson null.
ALGORITHM.md labels every step: exact, lossless, bounded or heuristic. The sibling bounds are lossless; gene retrieval is the heuristic step, and its z = 2 margin was measured.
Speed tricks · hardware

Sixteen to thirty-two alignments at once

Each lane runs the same Gotoh recurrence on a different read–allele job. The kernel is written once and compiled for AVX-512 (32 lanes), AVX2, ARM NEON and WebAssembly SIMD (16 lanes).

Inter-sequence SIMD: one CPU instruction updates the same cell of 16 or 32 different matrices, in 16-bit integers.

Reference profiles: each allele gets a precomputed table score[base][position], so the inner loop has no branches.

Batching: each stage runs over 64 reads at a time, yet every decision is per read. A read's result does not depend on its batch (tested).

Exactness: SIMD output is bit-identical to the scalar kernel, and the scalar kernel equals a textbook Gotoh. Output is byte-identical on x86-64 and ARM.

Putting V, D and J together

A short D must pay for every place it could have been

Search 32 D genes at 25 junction positions and a 5-base exact match turns up in random sequence all the time. So a D is only added if its score beats a prior cost for the look-elsewhere effect:

P*D = ln(N · L) / λ

For human IGH, N = 32 genes, L = 25 positions: P*D = 10.55. An exact 5-mer (score 10) loses; an exact 6-mer (12) wins. V and J pay the same kind of presence cost.

TRB has only 2 D genes, so P*D ≈ 6.2. The cost is derived from the number of hypotheses, not tuned.

Random junctions vs 6 real D genes

The demo uses only six IGHD genes, so chance hits are rarer than with all 32. The point is the shape: chance scores pile up at 6-10, which is why the threshold sits just above 10.

Putting V, D and J together · the novelty

One graph decides alleles and boundaries together

Nodes are placements: candidate alignments of an allele to a read interval (top 3 V, up to 32 D, top 3 J). Paths follow the grammar V → D(0 or 1) → J.

Between neighbours there are two cuts: V keeps bases before its exit, D keeps bases from its entry. Bases between the cuts are N/P. Overlaps can be split anywhere.

Try "My cuts" and move the sliders: every combination is a different hypothesis with its own score. The DAG finds the maximum over all of them, all alleles and all paths, exactly. Here it recovers the simulated truth base for base.
Putting V, D and J together

Exact, and linear in the overlaps

1 · Any clip in O(1)

Store prefix sums of each alignment's column scores. Keeping only bases [entry, exit) scores P[exit] − P[entry]: two lookups, one subtraction.

2 · Separable cuts

That score has one term per cut. So the best entry for each exit is a running maximum along the placement, and each edge costs O(overlap + 1) instead of trying all cut pairs.

3 · Order by kind

Nodes are sorted V, then D, then J, not by read start. A D may precede a J whose alignment reaches back over it, which is precisely the "J eats D" configuration.

score(path) = Σ clipped segment scores
          − PV·#V − PD·#D − PJ·#J

ties: fewer D first, then germline support

Lemma. Clipping an optimal local alignment never raises its score: every prefix and suffix scores ≥ 0, or the alignment would not have been optimal.

Theorem (checked by brute force). The DAG returns the optimum over all grammatical paths, placements and cut pairs. Tests compare it with exhaustive enumeration on simulated and hypermutated reads, including VDDJ grammars.

Earlier versions searched only inside overlaps and sorted by read start. Adversarial review and hypermutated test reads found both gaps; the exhaustive oracle now guards them.
This is a semi-Markov Viterbi: segments of variable length, scored as wholes, with the cut positions as the hidden states.
Putting V, D and J together

From the best answer to how sure we are

Posterior probabilities

The path score is a log-likelihood ratio, so it defines a distribution over every path and every cut pair:

P(path | read) = escore / Σ escore

Forward–backward over the same DAG (sum instead of max) gives the probability of each V, D, J and C allele and of "this read has a D". The best path is a mode of this distribution. Calibration is checked on simulated reads (ECE, Brier score).

Significance gates

Each V and J hit gets a Karlin–Altschul E-value, the number of chance hits this good:

E = K · m · n · e−λS
  • A hit is kept only if E ≤ 1.
  • A read is called only if its path holds an anchor: a V or J with E ≤ 10⁻³.

Ground truth

Scoring fairly against the truth

Equivalence

Germline-equivalent alleles

If two alleles differ only in bases that were trimmed away, no method could tell them apart. A call is correct when it contains the trimmed, pre-mutation germline that was simulated.

Pairing

Same reads, paired tests

Two tools run on identical reads. For each metric: the difference with a 95 % interval and an exact McNemar test on the reads only one tool gets right.

D honesty

D is reported two ways

Calling a D everywhere raises D accuracy. So D accuracy always appears next to the share of D-free reads left without a D, and split by remnant length (1–3, 4–7, 8+ nt).

The junction truth comes from the germline annotation. A wrong anchor relabels the truth instead of showing up as an error, so claims always compare two callers on one truth.
A remnant of 1-3 nt cannot be found by any method (below the 4-nt admission run), so accuracy there partly rests on luck.

Swig against IgBLAST 1.22

Swig IgBLAST

5,000 simulated human IGH reads per preset, forward strand. Accuracy = germline-equivalent allele; junction = exact CDR3 nucleotides. IgBLAST's auxiliary file lacked three J alleles; they were added so it could report junctions fairly.

Honest reading: on easy and mid reads both tools are close to the ceiling. The gaps open on hard reads: V +0.3, D +1.25, J +1.0, junction +1.6 points. IgBLAST's mid D accuracy is 0.4 points higher, but it gives a D to 43 % of D-free reads against Swig's 23 %, and its V gene is 0.06 points better on hard reads.

Wall clock for the whole command, posteriors on. Swig ≈ 5,100–5,200 reads/s; IgBLAST 66–71 reads/s.

D called on reads that have no D

Lower is better. A tool can buy D accuracy by calling D everywhere; Swig's derived D cost keeps this down while matching or beating D accuracy.

The speed comes from seeding and banding, lossless pruning, SIMD lanes and batching; the specificity comes from the derived D prior and the E-value gates.

What we do not claim

Limits and open questions

  • All benchmarks are simulated. The priors were measured on the same simulator. A real-data comparison (and partis) is still to do.
  • D on D-free reads: 16–24 % of reads whose D was fully deleted still get one (IgBLAST 30–43 %). On TRB the D cost is about 6, so one exact 4-mer clears it.
  • Junction gains versus the old core are coverage, not more precise boundaries.
  • Open: a prior on N/P length. The posterior's remaining miscalibration tracks junction length.
  • Open: one divergence per read assumes uniform mutation across V, D and J.
  • Not simulated: chimeras, long adapters, unseeded indels longer than 8 nt. Some old safeguards against them were removed.
These are taken from the benchmark limitations and ALGORITHM.md section 8 (open problems).
Take-home

Five ideas to remember

Scores are log-odds

+2/−3 is DNA's BLOSUM, re-read at each read's own divergence.

Local + affine

Smith–Waterman picks the piece; Gotoh prices indels as single events.

Seeds, then bands

2-bit k-mers vote on diagonals; DP runs only near them.

One exact DAG

Alleles and boundaries are chosen together, so neither V nor J eats the D.

Known truth

Simulated reads with per-base origin make every claim testable.
